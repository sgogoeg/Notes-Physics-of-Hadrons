\section{Baryon Spectroscopy}
\label{sec:baryon-spectroscopy}

The quark model of Chapter~\ref{sec:baryons-color} organizes the baryons into the
$\mathrm{SU}(6)\times O(3)$ supermultiplets and predicts, band by band, which excited
states should exist. Turning that prediction into physics requires confronting it with the
measured spectrum, and this is far from straightforward: the excited nucleons are broad,
they overlap strongly, and many of the states the quark model predicts have never been
seen. This chapter follows the experimental program that has been built to meet this
challenge---photoproduction of mesons off polarized nucleons, the measurement of
polarization observables, and their combination in a multichannel partial wave
analysis---and summarizes what the resulting spectrum has taught us, and where it still
resists understanding. The theoretical classification is taken as established from
Chapter~\ref{sec:baryons-color}; here the emphasis is on how the states are found and on
the questions they raise \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

% ============================================================================
\subsection{Motivation: baryons as bound states of QCD}
\label{subsec:why-baryons}

Baryons are the dominant part of the visible matter in the universe, and they played a
decisive role in its early development. Roughly one hundredth of a millisecond after the
Big Bang the universe cooled through the transition from a soup of deconfined quarks and
gluons---the quark--gluon plasma---to color-neutral hadrons, and the details of that
transition depend on the spectrum of baryon resonances that were then accessible. The
existence of baryons such as the $\Delta^{++}$, whose totally symmetric $\ket{uuu}$
configuration forced the introduction of the color quantum number, is itself a direct
manifestation of the non-abelian character of QCD \cite[Ch.~13]{Amsler2018}. It is
therefore hard to claim that quantum chromodynamics is understood while its own bound
states are not; the spectroscopy of baryons remains one of the least understood areas of
the Standard Model.

The central puzzle is how QCD builds heavy, massive bound states out of almost massless
quarks. The discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC established the mechanism that gives
the elementary quarks their \emph{current} masses, but this accounts for only a small
fraction of the nucleon mass. The light-quark current masses are of order a few
$\mathrm{MeV}$, whereas the effective \emph{constituent} masses needed to reproduce the
hadron spectrum are of order a few hundred $\mathrm{MeV}$, as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:quark-masses}. The proton mass of $938~\mathrm{MeV}$ corresponds to three
constituent quarks of roughly $300~\mathrm{MeV}$ each; the constituent mass is generated by
QCD itself, not by the Higgs mechanism. Even in the chiral limit of exactly massless $u$
and $d$ quarks more than eighty percent of the proton mass is still reproduced, a
phenomenon that F.~Wilczek aptly termed ``mass without mass'' \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

    \caption{Current (valence) quark masses generated by the Higgs mechanism, compared with

    constituents.}
    \label{tab:quark-masses}
\end{table}

Understanding the baryon spectrum therefore comes down to two questions that organize the
whole subject. The first concerns the relevant effective degrees of freedom: are they
constituent quarks, and if so how is their mass generated, or must one instead invoke
quark--diquark, meson--baryon, hybrid or multiquark configurations? The second concerns the
effective forces between those degrees of freedom---an effective one-gluon exchange, a
Goldstone-boson exchange, or instanton-induced interactions, all of which have been used
within quark models. Whether QCD can be approximated at all by constituent quarks plus a
linear confinement potential plus a residual interaction, and how confinement itself is
generated, are the open problems that motivate the measurements described in the remainder
of this chapter \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Quark-model predictions and the residual interaction}
\label{subsec:qm-predictions}

The starting point is the classification established in
Chapter~\ref{sec:baryons-color}. The totally antisymmetric color singlet factors out,
\begin{equation}
    \psi = \Phi_{\text{flavour}}\,\chi_{\text{spin}}\,\lambda_{\text{color}}\,R(\vec{r}),
\end{equation}
so that the remaining space--spin--flavor part must be symmetric. Coupling three
$\mathrm{SU}(6)$ fundamental representations gives
$\mathbf{6}\otimes\mathbf{6}\otimes\mathbf{6}
= \mathbf{56}_S\oplus\mathbf{70}_M\oplus\mathbf{70}_M\oplus\mathbf{20}_A$, and the orbital
motion, described by the two Jacobi coordinates $\vec\rho$ and $\vec\lambda$, is organized
by the harmonic-oscillator band number
\begin{equation}
    N = 2(n_\rho + n_\lambda) + \ell_\rho + \ell_\lambda,
    \qquad
    P = (-1)^{\ell_\rho + \ell_\lambda},
\end{equation}
with total orbital angular momentum $L = \ell_\rho + \ell_\lambda$. A supermultiplet is
labeled $(D, L^{P}_{N})$, where $D$ is the dimension of the $\mathrm{SU}(6)$ representation.
The ground state occupies the $(\mathbf{56},0^{+})$ at $N=0$, containing the
spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ octet and the spin-$\tfrac{3}{2}$ decuplet, and the lowest orbital
excitations form the $(\mathbf{70},1^{-})$ at $N=1$ \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}. Individual
states carry the modern name $N(\text{mass})J^{P}$; the older $L_{2I\,2J}(\text{mass})$
notation from $\pi N$ scattering, in which $L$ is the orbital angular momentum in the $\pi
N$ system, survives in labels such as $S_{11}(1535)$, now written $N(1535)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$.

The harmonic oscillator alone leaves each band degenerate and cannot reproduce the
observed masses; a residual interaction is needed to split the levels. Concrete quark
models assume that constituent quarks move in an effective confinement potential and add a
residual interaction of one of three kinds, before solving the equation of motion
\cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}. In the effective \emph{one-gluon exchange} picture the
non-relativistic model of Isgur and Karl fixes the color-hyperfine strength to the
$N$--$\Delta$ splitting; the spin--orbit ($\vec L\cdot\vec S$) term that this exchange also
predicts turns out to be large, in conflict with the data, and is set to zero by hand. The
relativized model of Capstick and Roberts retains the spin--orbit force but finds it
largely compensated by the Thomas precession. The \emph{Goldstone-boson (pion) exchange}
model of Glozman and Riska instead takes the spin--spin interaction into account while
neglecting the tensor piece. The \emph{instanton-induced} interaction of Kretzschmer,
L\"oring, Metsch and Petry is implemented in a fully relativistic quark model. Each of
these residual interactions is capable of reproducing the gross structure of the spectrum,
but they differ in detail and none is derived from first principles.

The simplest illustration is the first orbital excitation in the Isgur--Karl picture,
sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:isgur-karl}. Promoting the system from $L=0$ to $L=1$ costs one
oscillator quantum $\hbar\omega_0$, about $500~\mathrm{MeV}$, and the negative-parity
$N=1$ band splits under the residual and spin--orbit interactions into the five nucleon
levels $N(1535)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$, $N(1520)\tfrac{3}{2}^-$, $N(1650)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$,
$N(1675)\tfrac{5}{2}^-$ and $N(1700)\tfrac{3}{2}^-$, accompanied by two $\Delta$ states
with $J^{P}=\tfrac{1}{2}^-$ and $\tfrac{3}{2}^-$. The spin-dependent interaction
$V_{\text{spin}}$ is also what lifts the $\Delta(1232)$ above the nucleon in the ground
state \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

When the calculation is carried to higher bands, the models reproduce the overall pattern
of the $N^{*}$ and $\Delta^{*}$ spectra reasonably well, but two systematic problems
appear. The ordering of the lowest positive- and negative-parity states near
$1500~\mathrm{MeV}$---the inversion in which the Roper resonance
$N(1440)\tfrac{1}{2}^+$ lies below $N(1535)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$---is not reproduced by the
one-gluon-exchange models, and is better described once instanton-induced forces are
included. More seriously, all of these models predict many more states, especially at
higher masses, than have ever been observed. This is the long-standing
\emph{missing-resonance problem}. One popular response is that a baryon may possess an
effective quark--diquark structure, in which two of the quarks are frozen into a tightly
correlated pair; with one internal degree of freedom removed, the number of predicted
excitations is substantially reduced, bringing prediction closer to observation
\cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Excited baryons from lattice QCD}
\label{subsec:lattice-baryons}

Beyond quark models, the excitation spectrum can be computed directly from QCD on a
space--time lattice. The calculation of Edwards \textit{et al.}
(Phys.\ Rev.\ D 84 (2011) 074508) yields the excited $N^{*}$ and $\Delta^{*}$ spectra,
expressed in units of the $\Omega$ mass, ordered by $J^{P}$. The result is encouraging in
two respects that bear directly on the quark-model discussion of the previous subsection.
First, the spectrum exhibits the broad features expected from
$\mathrm{SU}(6)\otimes O(3)$ symmetry, and the counting of levels in each $J^{P}$ column
agrees with the non-relativistic quark model---the same rich pattern of states, including
those not yet observed experimentally, is present. Second, the lattice spectrum shows no
sign of parity doubling: states of opposite parity are not degenerate, a point to which we
return in Sect.~\ref{subsec:parity-doublets}. These lattice results are of course obtained
with approximations, most notably an unphysically large pion mass of
$m_\pi = 396~\mathrm{MeV}$, so they should be read as confirming the qualitative structure
rather than the precise masses \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{The excitation spectrum and the experimental challenge}
\label{subsec:excitation-challenge}

Like any composite system, a baryon reveals its internal dynamics through its excitation
spectrum. The idea is the same one that runs through atomic and nuclear physics, only the
energy scale changes: the atom, of size $10^{-10}~\mathrm{m}$, has excitations of order
$\mathrm{eV}$; the nucleus, of size $10^{-14}~\mathrm{m}$, of order $\mathrm{MeV}$; and the
proton, of size $10^{-15}~\mathrm{m}$, of order $\mathrm{GeV}$. In the atom the discrete
excitation energies show up directly as sharp absorption and emission lines, and the
spectrum can be read off almost by inspection. Studying the strong interaction through the
excitation spectrum of the proton and neutron---the lightest $qqq$ systems---is the direct
analogue of this atomic spectroscopy \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The analogy breaks down in one decisive respect. The excited nucleons do not appear as
sharp lines. Because they decay through the strong interaction their lifetimes are
extremely short, and Heisenberg's uncertainty relation $\Delta E\cdot\Delta t\gtrsim\hbar$
turns short lifetimes into large widths: the resonances are broad and strongly overlapping,
as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:broad-resonances}. What one measures is not a series of
isolated peaks but the coherent sum of many overlapping amplitudes, in which neighboring
states interfere. Extracting the individual resonances---their masses, widths, spins and
parities---from such a spectrum is the central experimental challenge of baryon
spectroscopy, and it is what makes the measurement of additional observables, discussed in
the following subsections, indispensable.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{Figures/broad_overlapping_resonances.pdf}
    \caption{The excited nucleons do not appear as the narrow, isolated lines one might
    naively expect (top), but as broad, strongly overlapping resonances whose amplitudes
    add coherently (bottom). Heisenberg's uncertainty relation converts the short strong-decay
    lifetimes into large widths, and the interference of neighboring states makes the
    individual resonances hard to disentangle.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Photoproduction experiments and the Crystal Barrel/TAPS setup at ELSA}
\label{subsec:photoproduction-elsa}

To disentangle the overlapping resonances one needs both a good understanding of the
spectrum and detailed knowledge of the properties, in particular the decays, of each
state. Two requirements follow directly: different final states must be investigated,
since a given resonance couples differently to each, and polarization observables must be
measured, since only these provide the information needed for an unambiguous partial wave
analysis. A vigorous experimental program pursuing exactly this has been underway
worldwide, at CLAS (Jefferson Lab), CBELSA/TAPS (ELSA), the Crystal Ball (MAMI) and LEPS
(SPring-8), all performing photoproduction experiments with polarized beams and polarized
targets \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The Crystal Barrel/TAPS experiment at the ELSA accelerator in Bonn is representative. An
electron beam extracted from ELSA passes through a goniometer and produces a
bremsstrahlung photon beam, whose energy is determined event by event by a magnetic
tagging system. The tagged photons strike a polarized target of butanol
($\mathrm{C_4H_9OH}$) held at $50~\mathrm{mK}$, and the reaction products are detected over
almost the full solid angle by the CsI(Tl) Crystal Barrel calorimeter together with an
inner detector, a forward detector, the TAPS calorimeter and a gas Cherenkov counter, with
a photon-intensity monitor downstream. This arrangement is well suited to reactions such as
$\gamma p \to N^{*} \to p\eta \to p\gamma\gamma$, in which the recoiling proton is tracked
while the decay photons are measured in the calorimeters, following the invariant-mass and
missing-mass techniques of Chapter~\ref{sec:invariant-mass} \cite[Ch.~2]{Amsler2018}.

The quality of the resulting data is high. In $\gamma p \to p4\gamma$ the experiment
resolves the $\pi^0\pi^0$, $\pi^0\eta$ and $\eta\eta$ combinations of the four photons with
low background and good resolution, giving clean access to $\gamma p \to p\pi^0\pi^0$ and
$\gamma p \to p\pi^0\eta$. These two-meson final states are especially valuable because
they permit the study of \emph{cascading} decays, in which a high-lying $N^{*}$ or
$\Delta^{*}$ decays by successive single-meson emission through an intermediate resonance,
as sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}. Each step of such a chain carries information about
a different resonance, so a single reaction can constrain several states at once.

\subsection{Separating overlapping resonances: final states and angular distributions}
\label{subsec:final-states-angles}

The total photoabsorption cross section on the proton, $\gamma p \to X$, shows only a few
broad bumps that flatten out with increasing photon energy; the individual resonances are
buried in the coherent sum and cannot be separated from the total cross section alone.
Additional information is needed, and the first handle is the choice of final state. A given
resonance couples with different strength to $p\pi^0$, $p\eta$, $p\pi^0\pi^0$ and
$p\pi^0\eta$, so the excitation functions of these exclusive channels peak at different
masses; comparing them already reveals that different states dominate in different regions,
which the inclusive cross section hides \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The second handle is the angular distribution. Even within a single final state, the
differential cross section $\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}\Omega$ carries information about the
spins and parities of the contributing resonances. For $\gamma p \to p\pi^0$, for example,
the distribution in $\cos\theta$ (with $\theta$ the polar angle of the emitted meson in the
center-of-mass frame, Fig.~\ref{fig:cm-geometry}) changes character markedly with photon
energy---forward--backward peaked at one energy, central at another---because different
partial waves, and hence different $J^{P}$, dominate. One caveat must be kept throughout:
not only resonant but also non-resonant background amplitudes contribute to the observed
distributions, and the two interfere, so the angular shapes cannot be read off as those of
a single state in isolation \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

    \centering

\subsection{Polarization observables and the complete experiment}
\label{subsec:polarization-observables}

Cross sections and angular distributions are not enough. For the photoproduction of a
pseudoscalar meson, $\gamma p \to p M$, the reaction is described by four complex
amplitudes, and reconstructing them---up to an overall phase---from the data requires a
carefully chosen set of observables. Chiang and Tabakin (Phys.\ Rev.\ C 55 (1997) 2054)
showed that eight well-chosen observables suffice to determine the contributing amplitudes,
and this set is the basis for an unambiguous partial wave analysis
\cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The consequence of measuring too few observables is not merely a loss of precision but a
genuine ambiguity. A well-documented example is $\gamma p \to p\eta$, where two different
partial wave solutions were found that both described the differential cross section
$\mathrm{d}\sigma/\mathrm{d}\Omega$ and the beam asymmetry $\Sigma$ equally well, yet
implied quite different resonance content---one solution dominated by
$N(1520)\tfrac{3}{2}^-$, the other by $N(1720)\tfrac{3}{2}^+$
(Elsner \textit{et al.}, Eur.\ Phys.\ J.\ A 33 (2007) 147). The two fits are
indistinguishable on the measured observables but describe different physics, so the
solution is not unique. Resolving such ambiguities requires further polarization
observables, which is the motivation for the double-polarization experiments of the next
subsection \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Double-polarization experiments as a filter for resonances}
\label{subsec:double-polarization}

The most powerful of the additional observables come from \emph{double-polarization}
experiments, which act as a filter for resonances. Using circularly polarized photons on a
longitudinally polarized target, one measures the two helicity cross sections
$\sigma_{1/2}$ and $\sigma_{3/2}$, defined by whether the photon spin and the nucleon spin
are antiparallel (total helicity $\tfrac{1}{2}$) or parallel (total helicity $\tfrac{3}{2}$),
as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:helicity-config}. Their difference isolates the
contributions of definite helicity and is conveniently expressed through the asymmetry
\begin{equation}
    E = \frac{\sigma_{1/2} - \sigma_{3/2}}{\sigma_{1/2} + \sigma_{3/2}}.
    \label{eq:E-observable}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering

        photon/.style={decorate, decoration={snake, amplitude=0.7pt, segment length=5pt}}]

            \draw[->] (-0.55,0) -- (0.35,0);          % photon spin ->
            \node[above] at (-0.1,0.05) {\footnotesize spin $1$};
            \draw[<-] (0.6,0) -- (1.5,0);             % nucleon spin <-
            \node[below] at (1.05,-0.05) {\footnotesize $-\tfrac{1}{2}$};
            \node at (0.5,-0.95) {$\sigma_{1/2}$};
        \end{scope}
        % right configuration: sigma_3/2
        \begin{scope}[xshift=6.2cm]
            \draw[photon] (-2.2,0) -- (-0.6,0);
            \draw[->] (-0.55,0) -- (0.35,0);          % photon spin ->
            \node[above] at (-0.1,0.05) {\footnotesize spin $1$};
            \draw[->] (0.6,0) -- (1.5,0);             % nucleon spin ->
            \node[below] at (1.05,-0.05) {\footnotesize $+\tfrac{1}{2}$};
            \node at (0.5,-0.95) {$\sigma_{3/2}$};
        \end{scope}
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{The two helicity configurations measured with a circularly polarized photon
    (spin $1$) and a longitudinally polarized nucleon. When the spins are antiparallel the
    total helicity is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ ($\sigma_{1/2}$, left); when they are parallel it is
    $\tfrac{3}{2}$ ($\sigma_{3/2}$, right). Their difference filters resonances by
    helicity.}
    \label{fig:helicity-config}
\end{figure}

For $\gamma p \to p\eta$ the helicity-difference counting rate $N_{1/2} - N_{3/2}$, and the
corresponding $\sigma_{1/2}-\sigma_{3/2}$, are dominated at threshold by the
$N(1535)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$ and provide direct evidence for the contributions of
$N(1710)\tfrac{1}{2}^+$ and $N(1720)\tfrac{3}{2}^+$ that the unpolarized data could not
fix (Müller \textit{et al.}, CBELSA/TAPS). For $\gamma p \to p\pi^0$ the separately measured
$\sigma_{1/2}$ and $\sigma_{3/2}$, together with the asymmetry $E$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:E-observable}, proved even more revealing: the established partial wave
analyses (SAID, MAID and Bonn--Gatchina), which all describe the previously existing
unpolarized photoproduction data, show large deviations from one another and from the new
data, even at low energies where everything was thought to be well understood. This
demonstrates the sensitivity of the double-polarization data to high-mass resonances
(Gottschall \textit{et al.}, CBELSA/TAPS, Phys.\ Rev.\ Lett.\ 112 (2014) 012003). The
double-polarization observables are, in short, the additional information the field had
been missing \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Results: new baryon resonances and the modern spectrum}
\label{subsec:new-resonances}

The high-quality polarization data, analyzed within the multichannel Bonn--Gatchina
partial wave analysis, have transformed the baryon spectrum. The analysis both confirms
known resonances---determining their properties more precisely---and establishes new ones.
The impact is striking when measured against the Review of Particle Physics: between 2000
and 2010 not a single new baryon resonance was added by the Particle Data Group, whereas
the photoproduction results of the past decade have introduced a whole series of states
and raised the status of others, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:bnga-states}. The reason
is that photoproduction provides access to the ``inelastic'' channels; before, the
database was built almost entirely from $\pi N$ scattering and some pion photoproduction.
Reaching the inelastic final states allows a much better determination of resonance
properties, and these results now enter the Review of Particle Physics directly
(Anisovich \textit{et al.}, Eur.\ Phys.\ J.\ A 48 (2012) 15; Müller \textit{et al.},
Phys.\ Lett.\ B 803 (2020) 135323) \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Parity doublets and the limits of present understanding}
\label{subsec:parity-doublets}

One pattern that emerges from the pole positions of the established $N^{*}$ and
$\Delta^{*}$ resonances is the appearance of \emph{parity doublets}: pairs of states of
equal spin and nearly equal mass but opposite parity. Their occurrence is not expected,
either by present lattice QCD calculations---which, as noted in
Sect.~\ref{subsec:lattice-baryons}, show no parity doubling---or by constituent quark
models. That such doublets nevertheless occur is a clear sign that strong QCD is not yet
understood \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The natural question is whether \emph{all} high-mass states come in parity doublets, and
here the answer is negative. Among the $\Delta$ states near $1900~\mathrm{MeV}$ the positive-parity
levels $\Delta(1910)\tfrac{1}{2}^+$, $\Delta(1920)\tfrac{3}{2}^+$,
$\Delta(1905)\tfrac{5}{2}^+$ and $\Delta(1950)\tfrac{7}{2}^+$ have negative-parity partners
$\Delta(1900)\tfrac{1}{2}^-$, $\Delta(1940)\tfrac{3}{2}^-$ and $\Delta(1930)\tfrac{5}{2}^-$
at each spin except $\tfrac{7}{2}$. A dedicated multichannel search for the parity partner
of the well-established $\Delta(1950)\tfrac{7}{2}^+$ (a four-star state), combining the
$\pi^0 p$, $\pi^+ n$, $K\Sigma$, $\pi^0\pi^0 p$ and $\pi^0\eta p$ channels, finds a
$J^{P}=\tfrac{7}{2}^-$ state only at the significantly higher mass of about
$2200~\mathrm{MeV}$---confirming the one-star $\Delta(2200)\tfrac{7}{2}^-$ of the PDG---and
no partner near $1950~\mathrm{MeV}$ (Anisovich \textit{et al.}, Phys.\ Lett.\ B 766 (2017)
357). The conclusion is that certain states have parity partners and others do not, a
pattern that remains unexplained. The supporting fits use the CBELSA/TAPS and CLAS
polarization data, with and without the $\Delta(2200)\tfrac{7}{2}^-$ amplitude included,
to demonstrate that the data require this state \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{The strange-baryon sector: $\Lambda^{*}$ and $\Sigma^{*}$}
\label{subsec:strange-baryons}

The progress just described concerns the non-strange sector, where substantial advances
have been made. The strange-baryon sector tells a very different story. The
$\mathrm{SU}(6)\times O(3)$ classification of Chapter~\ref{sec:baryons-color} predicts, in
addition to the $N^{*}$ and $\Delta^{*}$ multiplets, the octets, decuplets and flavor
singlets ($\Lambda^{*}$) built from the three light flavors $u$, $d$ and $s$. Clarifying
whether these predicted states actually exist is the obvious next step, because the
systematics of the full $\mathrm{SU}(6)\times O(3)$ multiplets cannot be tested on the
non-strange states alone. Yet the strange sector is very poorly known: several open
questions---which expected $qqq$ states exist, why certain resonances have parity partners
while others do not, and whether the relevant degrees of freedom are three quarks,
quark--diquark or meson--baryon configurations---all point back to the need for strange-baryon
data \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

The situation is stark. The Review of Particle Physics of 2024 describes the field as
``starved for data'': the established $\Lambda^{*}$ and $\Sigma^{*}$ resonances have
remained essentially unchanged for more than thirty years, and in the $qqq$-systematics
tables many expected states are either unknown or supported only by one- or two-star
evidence. Not even all members of the first excitation band are known. There is one
celebrated exception. The $\Lambda(1405)$, long treated as a single resonance, is now
understood in the chiral unitary approach---which builds it dynamically from the
meson--baryon interaction---to possess a \emph{two-pole} structure, with poles near
$(m,\Gamma)\approx(1325,\,180)~\mathrm{MeV}$ and $(1429,\,24)~\mathrm{MeV}$ rather than a
single state (Mai, Eur.\ Phys.\ J.\ Spec.\ Top.\ 230 (2021) 1593). The $\Lambda(1405)$ is
thus two states and not one, a reminder that even the best-studied strange baryon holds
surprises \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Outlook: INSIGHT at ELSA and the link to pentaquark spectroscopy}
\label{subsec:insight-outlook}

To supply the missing strange-baryon data a new experiment, INSIGHT (Investigation of the
Strong Interaction in the light-flavor sector), is being set up at ELSA as an integral part
of the Cluster of Excellence ``Color meets Flavor.'' It combines a polarized target, a
silicon-pixel vertex detector, the Crystal Barrel calorimeter, a magnet and the forward
endcap calorimeter of the PANDA experiment (PANDA-FWEC-EMC), together with a tagging
system, gas electron multiplier detectors, straw-tube trackers and a time-of-flight wall.
Covering almost the full $4\pi$ solid angle, it provides high-resolution photon detection,
precise charged-particle tracking and both a polarized beam and a polarized target, opening
access to new final states that include strange baryons \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

Isospin-selective reactions are the key. Channels such as
$\gamma p \to K^{+}\Lambda^{*} \to K^{+}\Sigma^0\pi^0$ and
$\gamma p \to K^{+}\Sigma^{*0} \to K^{+}\Lambda\pi^0$ single out the $\Lambda^{*}$ or the
$\Sigma^{*}$ by their isospin, and simulations show that states such as $\Lambda(1405)$ and
$\Lambda(1520)$ can be extracted with convincing signal-to-background even in the presence
of the many competing channels, with a $\pi^0$ mass resolution of a few $\mathrm{MeV}$ and
particle identification from time of flight. The measurement of polarization observables is
essential here for the same reason as in the non-strange sector: several groups have
reanalyzed the existing strange-baryon data (from KSU, ANL/Osaka, Bonn--Gatchina and JPAC)
and obtained different solutions from almost the same data, which the target asymmetry
accessible with a polarized target can distinguish \cite[Ch.~15]{Amsler2018}.

Well-determined strange-baryon resonances are important beyond spectroscopy proper. In the
LHCb discovery of hidden-charm pentaquark candidates in
$\Lambda_b^0 \to J/\psi\, p\, K^{-}$---the $P_c(4312)^+$, $P_c(4440)^+$ and
$P_c(4457)^+$---the $K^{-}p$ system proceeds through a dense spectrum of $\Lambda^{*}$
resonances, and the properties of those resonances directly affect the extraction of the
pentaquark parameters. Whether the $P_c$ states are genuine compact pentaquarks or
meson--baryon molecules is still open, and polarization experiments that pin down the
strange-baryon background are needed to avoid ambiguities; a common analysis of ELSA and
LHCb data would be a considerable advantage. The pentaquark states themselves are treated in
detail in Chapter~\ref{sec:multiquark} \cite[Ch.~16]{Amsler2018}. With INSIGHT the light
strange-baryon spectrum is finally within reach, and the next chapter turns from the
spectrum of hadrons to their internal structure as probed by electron scattering
(Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}).
