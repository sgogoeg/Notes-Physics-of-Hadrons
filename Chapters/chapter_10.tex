\section{Deep inelastic scattering and the multidimensional structure of the nucleon}
\label{sec:dis-nucleon-structure}

Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering} treated the two regimes in which the nucleon
responds to a virtual photon as a coherent object: elastic scattering, where the target
recoils intact and its response is a handful of form factors, and resonance
electroproduction, where the photon excites a definite state described by transition
form factors. Both descriptions break down once the momentum transfer becomes large
enough to resolve distances small compared with the nucleon itself. Then the photon no
longer scatters off the proton as a whole but off its pointlike constituents, the
hadronic final state is a jet of many particles rather than a single baryon, and the
natural language is no longer form factors but the momentum, spin and transverse-motion
\emph{distributions} of the quarks and gluons inside the nucleon. This chapter follows
that transition. It begins with inclusive deep inelastic scattering and the structure
functions, works through the parton model and its scaling violations, and then turns to
the frontiers where a one-dimensional momentum picture is no longer enough: the spin
decomposition of the nucleon, its three-dimensional structure in momentum space, and
finally its spatial structure, reached through exclusive reactions and generalized
parton distributions. The closing subsections assemble all of these objects into a
single hierarchy and sketch what remains to be measured
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}. The standard monograph on the subject, Thomas and Weise
\cite{ThomasWeise2001}, is used throughout as the principal reference for the
formalism; its Chapters~2--4 follow the same progression from elastic form factors
through weak probes to deep inelastic scattering that these two chapters take.

\subsection{Kinematics of inclusive scattering and the hadronic tensor}
\label{subsec:inclusive-kinematics}

The measurement that opens the deep inelastic regime is the simplest one imaginable:
detect only the scattered electron and sum over everything else. The reaction is
\begin{equation}
    e(k) + N(P) \;\rightarrow\; e(k') + X,
    \label{eq:inclusive-reaction}
\end{equation}
where $X$ stands for any hadronic final state allowed by conservation laws, and the
cross section is the incoherent sum over all such states,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{incl}} \;=\; \sum_{X}\,\bigl|\mathcal{M}(eN\to eX)\bigr|^{2}.
    \label{eq:sigma-incl}
\end{equation}
The kinematics are exactly those introduced in Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:kinematics}), now used in a new region. The virtual photon carries
four-momentum $q=k-k'$ and virtuality $Q^{2}=-q^{2}$; in the target rest frame it
transfers energy
\begin{equation}
    \nu \;=\; E - E' \;=\; \frac{P\cdot q}{M},
\end{equation}
and the squared invariant mass of the hadronic system is
\begin{equation}
    W^{2} \;=\; (P+q)^{2} \;=\; M^{2} + 2M\nu - Q^{2}.
    \label{eq:W2-again}
\end{equation}
Two dimensionless variables organize the inclusive data. The Bjorken variable
\begin{equation}
    x_{B} \;=\; \frac{Q^{2}}{2P\cdot q} \;=\; \frac{Q^{2}}{2M\nu}
    \label{eq:xB-again}
\end{equation}
runs from $x_{B}=1$ at the elastic point down toward zero deep in the continuum, and
the inelasticity
\begin{equation}
    y \;=\; \frac{P\cdot q}{P\cdot k} \;=\; \frac{M\nu}{P\cdot k}
    \label{eq:inelasticity}
\end{equation}
measures the fraction of the electron energy transferred to the target in the rest
frame. The elastic limit is recovered when the target recoils as a single nucleon,
$W=M$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:W2-again} forces $Q^{2}=2M\nu$ and hence $x_{B}=1$; every
inelastic process has $W>M$ and $x_{B}<1$ \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018},
\cite[Ch.~4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The dynamical content of the reaction is separated from the kinematics in the same way
that was anticipated at the end of Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}. The amplitude
factorizes into a leptonic and a hadronic piece joined by the photon propagator,
\begin{equation}
    A \;\sim\; \frac{1}{q^{2}}\,j^{\mu}\,J_{\mu},
\end{equation}
where $j^{\mu}$ is the electron current, known exactly from quantum electrodynamics, and
$J_{\mu}$ is the hadronic current, which we cannot compute. Squaring the amplitude and
summing over final states, the cross section is a contraction of a \emph{leptonic
tensor} $L_{\mu\nu}$ with a \emph{hadronic tensor} $W^{\mu\nu}$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}\sigma \;\sim\; |A|^{2} \;\sim\; L_{\mu\nu}\,W^{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
with the hadronic tensor defined by the current matrix elements summed over the
unobserved states $X$,
\begin{equation}
    2W^{\mu\nu} \;=\; \sum_{X}\,
    \bra{N(p)} J^{\mu\dagger} \ket{X}\bra{X} J^{\nu} \ket{N(p)}\,
    (2\pi)^{3}\,\delta^{(4)}(p+q-p_{X}).
    \label{eq:hadronic-tensor}
\end{equation}
The leptonic tensor is calculable; the hadronic tensor is not, because it contains the
full non-perturbative structure of the target. The strategy of the rest of this chapter
is the one used throughout hadron physics: since $W^{\mu\nu}$ cannot be calculated, it
is \emph{parametrized} by writing down the most general expression consistent with
Lorentz covariance, current conservation and parity, and the coefficient functions that
appear---the \emph{structure functions}---are then measured. A fuller derivation may be
found in the lecture reference (F.\,E.\ Close, \emph{An Introduction to Quarks and
Partons}) and in Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~4]{ThomasWeise2001}, whose Appendix~B
collects the corresponding lepton-- and photon--nucleon cross sections in explicit
form.

\subsection{Structure functions and the generalized Rosenbluth formula}
\label{subsec:structure-functions}

Carrying out the covariant decomposition of $W^{\mu\nu}$ for unpolarized scattering
yields two independent structure functions, conventionally $W_{1}$ and $W_{2}$, each a
function of the two invariants $Q^{2}$ and $\nu$. Contracting with the leptonic tensor
generalizes the elastic Rosenbluth formula of Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering} to
the inelastic case,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\mathrm{d}E'}
    = \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}\Omega}\right)_{\!\mathrm{Mott}}
      \left[\,W_{2}(Q^{2},\nu) + 2\,W_{1}(Q^{2},\nu)\tan^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\right],
    \label{eq:inelastic-rosenbluth}
\end{equation}
where the elastic form-factor combinations have been replaced by the two structure
functions, and, crucially, these are now functions of \emph{two} independent variables
rather than of $Q^{2}$ alone: at fixed $Q^{2}$ the target may be left in a continuum of
final-state masses. Just as in the elastic case, the coefficients of the Mott cross
section separate a longitudinal-like ($W_{2}$) from a transverse-like ($W_{1}$) response,
and they can be disentangled by varying the scattering angle at fixed $Q^{2}$ and $\nu$
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}. The covariant decomposition of $W^{\mu\nu}$ that produces
$W_{1}$ and $W_{2}$, and the cross-section formulae that follow from it, are given in
Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

It is more common to work with the dimensionless structure functions
\begin{align}
    F_{1}(x,Q^{2}) &= M\,W_{1}(Q^{2},\nu),
    \label{eq:F1-def}\\
    F_{2}(x,Q^{2}) &= \nu\,W_{2}(Q^{2},\nu),
    \label{eq:F2-def}
\end{align}
which absorb the kinematic mass and energy factors and, as the notation anticipates,
turn out to depend essentially on the single variable $x=x_{B}$. In terms of $F_{1}$ and
$F_{2}$, and changing variables to $Q^{2}$ and $x$, the cross section reads
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}
    = \frac{4\pi\alpha^{2}}{Q^{4}}
      \left[\left(1 - y - \frac{M^{2}y^{2}x^{2}}{Q^{2}}\right)
      \frac{F_{2}(x,Q^{2})}{x} + y^{2}F_{1}(x,Q^{2})\right].
    \label{eq:dis-xsec}
\end{equation}
Because $F_{1}$ and $F_{2}$ depend on two variables, a full determination requires
measurements at fixed $x$ and $Q^{2}$ but at several values of $\nu$---that is, at
several beam energies---so that the separation implied by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:inelastic-rosenbluth}
can be performed \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The several classes of electron-scattering measurement occupy distinct regions of the
$(\nu,Q^{2})$ plane, and it is worth mapping them out before interpreting the structure
functions, because the interpretation changes qualitatively from one region to the next.
Figure~\ref{fig:dis-phase-space} shows the standard picture. The line
$Q^{2}=2M\nu$---equivalently $W=M$, $x_{B}=1$---is elastic scattering; below and to the
left of it lies a kinematically forbidden region; along the $\nu$ axis at $Q^{2}=0$ sits
real-photon photoproduction; the band of resonances of
Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering} runs just below the elastic line; and the open
region of large $\nu$ and large $Q^{2}$, where $W\gg M$, is the deep inelastic
scattering region proper.

\subsection{The parton model and Bjorken scaling}
\label{subsec:parton-model}

The decisive experimental fact about the structure functions is that, in the deep
inelastic region, they cease to depend on $Q^{2}$ and become functions of $x$ alone.
This \emph{Bjorken scaling} is what one expects if the virtual photon, at high $Q^{2}$,
scatters elastically off pointlike, effectively free constituents inside the nucleon
rather than off an extended charge distribution: an extended object would impose a
$Q^{2}$-dependent form factor, whereas a point charge does not. The constituents are
Feynman's \emph{partons}, identified with the quarks (and, as it emerged, the gluons) of
the quark model \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4.1]{ThomasWeise2001}. A second
consequence of scattering off pointlike spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ constituents is the
Callan--Gross relation $F_{2}(x)=2xF_{1}(x)$, which ties the two structure functions
together and is well satisfied by the data; had the partons been spinless, $F_{1}$ would
have vanished instead \cite[Ch.~4.1]{ThomasWeise2001}.

In the parton model the structure function $F_{2}$ is a charge-weighted sum over the
quark momentum distributions,
\begin{equation}
    F_{2}(x) \;=\; x_{B}\sum_{q} e_{q}^{2}\,\bigl[q(x) + \bar q(x)\bigr],
    \label{eq:F2-parton}
\end{equation}
the sum running over quark flavors with electric charges $e_{q}$. Writing $u^{p}(x)$,
$d^{p}(x),\dots$ for the probability densities to find a $u$, $d,\dots$ quark carrying
longitudinal momentum fraction $x$ in the proton, and using $e_{u}=+\tfrac{2}{3}$,
$e_{d}=-\tfrac{1}{3}$ so that $e_{u}^{2}=\tfrac{4}{9}$ and $e_{d}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{9}$,
\begin{align}
    F_{2}^{p}(x) &= x\left(\tfrac{4}{9}\,u^{p}(x) + \tfrac{1}{9}\,d^{p}(x) + \cdots\right),
    \label{eq:F2p}\\
    F_{2}^{n}(x) &= x\left(\tfrac{1}{9}\,u^{p}(x) + \tfrac{4}{9}\,d^{p}(x) + \cdots\right),
    \label{eq:F2n}
\end{align}
where the neutron distributions have been related to the proton ones by isospin
symmetry, $u^{n}=d^{p}$ and $d^{n}=u^{p}$, which follows because the neutron is the
isospin partner of the proton ($I=\tfrac{1}{2}$, $I_{3}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$ versus
$+\tfrac{1}{2}$). Measuring $F_{2}$ on proton and neutron (deuteron) targets over the
full range of $x$ and $Q^{2}$ therefore separates the up- and down-quark distributions;
the accumulated data span many orders of magnitude in $Q^{2}$ and confirm scaling to
good approximation across most of the $x$ range \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018},
\cite[Ch.~4.5]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Neutrino scattering provides a complementary and charge-selective probe. The
charged-current reaction is mediated by the $W$ boson rather than the photon, so it
couples to a definite quark chirality and introduces a third, parity-violating structure
function $F_{3}$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma^{\nu,\bar\nu}}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y}
    = \frac{G_{F}^{2}\,s}{2\pi}
      \left[\,F_{1}^{(\nu,\bar\nu)}\,x y^{2}
      + F_{2}^{(\nu,\bar\nu)}(1-y)
      \pm F_{3}^{(\nu,\bar\nu)}\left(1-\tfrac{y}{2}\right)x y\,\right],
    \label{eq:neutrino-xsec}
\end{equation}
with the $+$ sign for neutrinos and the $-$ sign for antineutrinos. Because the $W$
selects quark flavors, the neutrino structure functions pick out specific combinations,
\begin{align}
    F_{2}^{\nu p} = F_{2}^{\bar\nu n} &= 2x\,[\,d(x) + \bar u(x)\,],
    & F_{2}^{\nu n} = F_{2}^{\bar\nu p} &= 2x\,[\,u(x) + \bar d(x)\,],
    \label{eq:F2-neutrino}\\
    F_{3}^{\nu p} = F_{3}^{\bar\nu n} &= 2\,[\,d(x) - \bar u(x)\,],
    & F_{3}^{\nu n} = F_{3}^{\bar\nu p} &= 2\,[\,u(x) - \bar d(x)\,],
    \label{eq:F3-neutrino}
\end{align}
so that combining charged-lepton and neutrino data cleanly disentangles the valence
distributions (through $F_{3}$, which is sensitive to $q-\bar q$) from the sea (through
the $q+\bar q$ combinations in $F_{2}$) \cite[Ch.~4.3]{ThomasWeise2001}. The first
moment of $F_{3}$ simply counts the valence quarks of the target, which is the content of
the Gross--Llewellyn-Smith sum rule \cite[Ch.~4.4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{QCD evolution: the DGLAP equations}
\label{subsec:dglap}

Bjorken scaling is only approximate. Inspected closely, the structure functions drift
with $Q^{2}$: at small $x$ they rise and at large $x$ they fall as $Q^{2}$ increases.
These \emph{scaling violations} are not a failure of the parton picture but a prediction
of quantum chromodynamics. As the resolution $Q^{2}$ is raised, the photon resolves ever
finer structure---a quark is seen to be accompanied by the gluons it radiates and the
quark--antiquark pairs those gluons produce---so the momentum distributions themselves
depend logarithmically on the scale at which they are probed. The evolution is governed
by the DGLAP (Dokshitzer--Gribov--Lipatov--Altarelli--Parisi) equations, which for the
quark and gluon distributions read
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial q_{i}(x,Q^{2})}{\partial\ln Q^{2}}
    &= \frac{\alpha_{s}(Q^{2})}{2\pi}\sum_{j}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y}
       \left[P_{q_{i}\leftarrow q_{j}}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)q_{j}(y,Q^{2})
       + P_{q_{i}\leftarrow g}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)g(y,Q^{2})\right],
    \label{eq:dglap-q}\\[4pt]
    \frac{\partial g(x,Q^{2})}{\partial\ln Q^{2}}
    &= \frac{\alpha_{s}(Q^{2})}{2\pi}\sum_{j}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{y}
       \left[P_{g\leftarrow q_{j}}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)q_{j}(y,Q^{2})
       + P_{g\leftarrow g}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{y}\right)g(y,Q^{2})\right].
    \label{eq:dglap-g}
\end{align}
The \emph{splitting functions} $P_{a\leftarrow b}(z)$ give the probability that a parton
of type $b$ radiates a parton of type $a$ carrying a fraction $z$ of its momentum; they
are calculable in perturbative QCD and correspond to the elementary vertices
$q\to qg$, $g\to q\bar q$ and $g\to gg$. Given the distributions measured at one scale,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dglap-q}--\eqref{eq:dglap-g} predict them at every other scale, and the
agreement of this evolution with the measured $Q^{2}$ dependence of $F_{2}$ over several
orders of magnitude is one of the most stringent quantitative tests of QCD. It is also
the only handle that inclusive electromagnetic DIS has on the gluon, which carries no
electric charge and is therefore invisible to the photon except through its effect on
the evolution \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4.2]{ThomasWeise2001}. A more formal
route to the same equations proceeds through the operator product expansion and the
renormalization group, which express the moments of the structure functions in terms of
anomalous dimensions; Thomas and Weise develop that machinery in full
\cite[Ch.~5]{ThomasWeise2001}.

An important consistency condition constrains the distributions at any scale: the total
longitudinal momentum of the nucleon must be shared among its partons,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\;x_{i}\,q_{i}(x) \;=\; 1,
    \label{eq:momentum-sum-rule}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all quarks, antiquarks and gluons. Experimentally the quarks and
antiquarks carry only about half of the nucleon momentum; the remainder is carried by
the gluons \cite[Ch.~4.4]{ThomasWeise2001}. Modern \emph{global fits}---for example the
MSHT20 analysis---determine the
full set of parton distributions by fitting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dglap-q}--\eqref{eq:dglap-g}
simultaneously to a large body of DIS, Drell--Yan and collider data, exploiting the fact
that each process constrains a particular flavor combination in a particular range of
$x$. Accurate parton distributions are not only of intrinsic interest: they are a
required input to essentially every cross-section prediction at the LHC, so that the
precision of collider physics rests directly on the quality of these fits.

\subsection{Spin structure of the nucleon: polarized DIS}
\label{subsec:polarized-dis}

Unpolarized DIS answers how the \emph{momentum} of the nucleon is distributed among its
partons. It says nothing about how the \emph{spin} is distributed, and that turns out to
be a subtler and historically more surprising question. To reach it one polarizes both
the beam and the target,
\begin{equation}
    \vec e(k) + \vec p(P) \;\rightarrow\; e(k') + X,
\end{equation}
and, in the simplest configuration, aligns the spins longitudinally---along the beam
direction---and compares the cross sections with the beam and target spins parallel and
antiparallel. The observable is the longitudinal double-spin asymmetry
\begin{equation}
    A_{LL} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^{\uparrow\uparrow} - \sigma^{\uparrow\downarrow}}
                       {\sigma^{\uparrow\uparrow} + \sigma^{\uparrow\downarrow}},
    \label{eq:ALL}
\end{equation}
in which the large spin-independent part of the cross section cancels in the difference,
leaving the spin-dependent response.

The hadronic tensor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hadronic-tensor} has an antisymmetric,
spin-dependent part that was dropped in the unpolarized treatment. Restoring it and
repeating the covariant decomposition introduces two new, spin-dependent structure
functions $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$, appearing in the polarized cross-section difference
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma(\uparrow\downarrow - \uparrow\uparrow)}
         {\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\mathrm{d}E'}
    = \frac{4\alpha^{2}E'}{E\,Q^{2}}
      \bigl\{(E + E'\cos\theta)\,M\,G_{1} - Q^{2}\,G_{2}\bigr\}.
    \label{eq:polarized-xsec}
\end{equation}
Exactly as $W_{1,2}$ were traded for the scaling functions $F_{1,2}$, the spin-dependent
structure functions are rewritten as
\begin{align}
    g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) &= M^{2}\nu\,G_{1}(\nu,Q^{2}),
    \label{eq:g1-def}\\
    g_{2}(x,Q^{2}) &= M\nu^{2}\,G_{2}(\nu,Q^{2}),
    \label{eq:g2-def}
\end{align}
and the polarized cross-section difference takes the compact scaling form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma(\uparrow\downarrow - \uparrow\uparrow)}
         {\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\Omega}
    = \frac{4\pi\alpha^{2}}{M E x\,Q^{2}}
      \left[\left(2 - \frac{Q^{2}}{2M E x} - \frac{Q^{2}}{2E^{2}}\right)
      g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) - \frac{2Mx}{E}\,g_{2}(x,Q^{2})\right].
    \label{eq:g1g2-xsec}
\end{equation}
The functions $g_{1}$ and $g_{2}$ are the spin-dependent structure functions; they carry
the information about how the parton spins are oriented inside a polarized nucleon
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The parton interpretation of $g_{1}$ parallels that of $F_{2}$. Introduce the quark
\emph{helicity distributions}, the difference between the density of quarks with spin
aligned and anti-aligned with the parent nucleon,
\begin{align}
    \Delta q(x) &= q^{\uparrow}(x) - q^{\downarrow}(x),
    \label{eq:delta-q}\\
    \Delta\bar q(x) &= \bar q^{\uparrow}(x) - \bar q^{\downarrow}(x),
    \label{eq:delta-qbar}
\end{align}
where the arrows denote the quark helicity relative to the nucleon spin. Then, in the
parton model,
\begin{equation}
    g_{1}(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{q} e_{q}^{2}\,
    \bigl[\Delta q(x) + \Delta\bar q(x)\bigr],
    \label{eq:g1-parton}
\end{equation}
the charge-weighted sum of the quark helicity distributions, in exact analogy with
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F2-parton}. Polarized DIS thus gives direct access to the helicity
structure of the partons, the spin analogue of the momentum distributions measured in
the unpolarized case \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}. The second spin structure function
$g_{2}$ has no such simple interpretation: it receives higher-twist contributions in
which the quarks cannot be treated as independent, so that it measures quark--gluon
correlations rather than a probability density \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{Measuring \texorpdfstring{$g_{1}$}{g1}: asymmetries, depolarization and
the EMC experiment}
\label{subsec:g1-measurement}

Measuring the polarized cross section directly is impractical, because it is a small
difference of two large numbers. In practice one measures asymmetries, in which the bulk
of the systematic uncertainties cancels. The measured lepton-level asymmetry is
\begin{equation}
    A(x,Q^{2})
    = \frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma^{\uparrow\downarrow}}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}
          - \dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma^{\uparrow\uparrow}}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}}
           {\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma^{\uparrow\downarrow}}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}
          + \dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma^{\uparrow\uparrow}}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^{2}}}
    = x y\,
      \frac{\left(2 - y - \dfrac{Mxy}{E}\right)g_{1} - \dfrac{2Mx}{E}\,g_{2}}
           {\left(1 - y - \dfrac{Mxy}{2E}\right)F_{2} + x y^{2}F_{1}}.
    \label{eq:A-lepton}
\end{equation}
It is not the electron but the exchanged virtual photon that probes the nucleon, and it
is often cleaner to work with the \emph{virtual-photon} asymmetries. These are defined
from the photoabsorption cross sections for the two transverse helicity states,
$\sigma^{T}_{1/2}$ and $\sigma^{T}_{3/2}$, the transverse--longitudinal interference
$\sigma_{TL}$, and the longitudinal-to-transverse ratio $R=\sigma_{L}/\sigma_{T}$,
\begin{equation}
    A_{1}(x,Q^{2}) = \frac{\sigma^{T}_{1/2} - \sigma^{T}_{3/2}}
                          {\sigma^{T}_{1/2} + \sigma^{T}_{3/2}},
    \qquad
    A_{2}(x,Q^{2}) = \frac{\sigma_{TL}}{\sigma_{T}},
    \label{eq:A1A2-def}
\end{equation}
and are related to the structure functions by
\begin{align}
    A_{1}(x,Q^{2}) &= \frac{2x(1+R)}{F_{2}(x,Q^{2})}
        \left[g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) - \frac{2Mx}{Ey}\,g_{2}(x,Q^{2})\right],
    \label{eq:A1}\\
    A_{2}(x,Q^{2}) &= \frac{2x(1+R)}{F_{2}(x,Q^{2})}
        \sqrt{\frac{2Mx}{Ey}}\,
        \bigl(g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) + g_{2}(x,Q^{2})\bigr).
    \label{eq:A2}
\end{align}
Inverting, the polarized structure function is
\begin{equation}
    g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) = \frac{F_{2}(x,Q^{2})}{2x\,\bigl(1 + R(x,Q^{2})\bigr)}
        \left[A_{1} + \sqrt{\frac{2Mx}{Ey}}\,A_{2}\right].
    \label{eq:g1-from-A}
\end{equation}
The measured asymmetry $A$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:A-lepton} is related to the photon
asymmetries through the \emph{depolarization factor} $D$, which measures how much of the
electron polarization is transferred to the virtual photon,
\begin{equation}
    A = D\,(A_{1} + \eta A_{2}) \approx D A_{1},
    \qquad
    D = \frac{y(2-y)}{y^{2} + 2(1-y)(1+R)},
    \qquad
    \eta = \frac{2(1-y)\sqrt{Q^{2}}}{Ey(2-y)},
    \label{eq:depolarization}
\end{equation}
where the kinematic coefficient $\eta$ is small over most of the accessible range, so
that $A_{2}$ contributes little. Combining these relations gives the working formula by
which $g_{1}$ is extracted from the measured asymmetry,
\begin{equation}
    g_{1}(x,Q^{2}) \;\approx\; \frac{F_{2}(x,Q^{2})}{2x\,\bigl(1 + R(x,Q^{2})\bigr)}\,
    \frac{A}{D}.
    \label{eq:g1-working}
\end{equation}

The classic measurement was made by the European Muon Collaboration (EMC) at the CERN M2
beamline---the same beamline now used by AMBER (Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}).
A high-energy muon beam scattered off a polarized target built from two cells polarized
in opposite directions, an arrangement that measures both spin orientations
simultaneously and cancels many of the systematic uncertainties associated with beam and
target drifts \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}.

The result was unexpected. The first moment of $g_{1}^{p}$ can be written
\begin{equation}
    \int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\;g_{1}^{p}(x,Q^{2})
    = a(Q^{2}) + b(Q^{2})\,\Delta\Sigma,
    \label{eq:g1-moment}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\Sigma$ is the total quark and antiquark helicity contribution to the
nucleon spin. The Ellis--Jaffe sum rule, which assumes a spin-symmetric sea and takes the
octet axial
charges from hyperon beta decay, predicted
$\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}^{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0.185$, corresponding to quarks carrying most of
the spin \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}. It is worth being clear about which assumption
failed: the closely related Bjorken sum rule, formed from the \emph{difference} of the
proton and neutron moments, rests only on isospin symmetry and the measured axial charge
$g_{A}/g_{V}$, and it is well confirmed---so the discrepancy lies in the flavor-singlet
part, not in the framework \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}. The EMC found instead
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Sigma = (14 \pm 3 \pm 10)\,\%,
    \label{eq:EMC-result}
\end{equation}
consistent with the quarks carrying almost \emph{none} of the nucleon spin---the
\emph{proton spin crisis}. Later, higher-statistics data, most notably from COMPASS,
raised the value to
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Sigma = (33 \pm 3 \pm 5)\,\%,
    \label{eq:COMPASS-result}
\end{equation}
still far below the naive expectation $\Delta\Sigma\sim 1$ that the proton spin is
carried entirely by the valence quarks.

\subsection{The proton spin sum rule: resolving the spin crisis}
\label{subsec:spin-sum-rule}

To interpret $\Delta\Sigma$ one needs a bookkeeping of all the ways angular momentum can
be stored in the nucleon. In this context the parton spin means the parton helicity in
the infinite-momentum frame, and the total spin of the nucleon decomposes as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\Sigma + \Delta G + L_{q} + L_{g},
    \label{eq:spin-sum-rule}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\Sigma$ is the total quark and antiquark helicity contribution, $\Delta G$
the gluon helicity contribution, and $L_{q}$ and $L_{g}$ the orbital angular momenta of
the quarks and gluons respectively. The naive expectation is $\Delta\Sigma\sim 1$, the
proton spin carried entirely by the valence-quark spins; the measured value
\eqref{eq:COMPASS-result} shows that this cannot be the whole story
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A large part of the deficit can be accounted for without invoking anything exotic. First,
the valence quarks are relativistic and cannot be treated as static: their lower Dirac
components carry orbital motion that reduces the fraction of the spin stored as quark
helicity from $100\%$ to roughly $65\%$. Second, the pion cloud---the nucleon spending
part of its time as a virtual baryon plus pion---dilutes the quark helicity by a further
factor of about $0.7$--$0.8$. Third, one-gluon exchange between the quarks lowers the
quark-spin fraction by a further $\sim 15\%$. Multiplying these effects together,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Sigma \;=\; (0.7\text{--}0.8)\times(0.65 - 0.15) \;\sim\; 35\text{--}40\,\%,
    \label{eq:spin-estimate}
\end{equation}
in reasonable agreement with the data. None of these three ingredients is an \emph{ad
hoc} repair: the relativistic reduction is built into the bag models, and the pion-cloud
dilution is a characteristic prediction of the cloudy bag and the other chiral quark
models \cite[Ch.~8]{ThomasWeise2001}, the pion cloud itself being a direct consequence of
spontaneously broken chiral symmetry \cite[Ch.~7]{ThomasWeise2001}. Some spin is still
unaccounted for, and the natural candidate is the gluon---as so often in nucleon
structure, the glue is called on to make up the balance.

\subsection{Gluon polarization and direct measurements}
\label{subsec:gluon-polarization}

The gluons carry roughly half of the nucleon momentum (Sect.~\ref{subsec:dglap}), so it
is natural to ask how much of its \emph{spin} they carry---the term $\Delta G$ in the sum
rule \eqref{eq:spin-sum-rule}. By analogy with the quark case one defines the gluon
helicity distribution
\begin{equation}
    \Delta g(x) \;=\; g^{\uparrow}(x) - g^{\downarrow}(x),
    \label{eq:delta-g}
\end{equation}
the difference between the densities of gluons with helicity aligned and anti-aligned
with the nucleon spin. Because the gluon carries no electric charge, it does not appear
directly in the photon-mediated $g_{1}$; it enters only indirectly, through its effect on
the scale evolution of the quark helicity distributions. The polarized DGLAP equation
couples $\Delta q$ to $\Delta g$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial\Delta q(x,Q^{2})}{\partial\ln Q^{2}}
    \;\sim\; \Delta P_{qq}\,\Delta q(x,Q^{2}) + \Delta P_{qg}\,\Delta g(x,Q^{2}),
    \label{eq:polarized-dglap}
\end{equation}
so a scaling-violation analysis of $g_{1}(x,Q^{2})$ constrains $\Delta g$, but only
weakly over the limited lever arm in $Q^{2}$ available in fixed-target DIS. There is a
further subtlety of principle here: the flavor-singlet axial current is not conserved at
the quantum level, and the axial anomaly mixes the quark and gluon contributions, so that
the split of Eq.~\eqref{eq:spin-sum-rule} into $\Delta\Sigma$ and $\Delta G$ is
scheme dependent and part of the apparent smallness of $\Delta\Sigma$ can be moved into
an anomalous gluon term \cite[Ch.~4.6]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A direct handle on $\Delta g$ comes from polarized proton--proton collisions, in which
gluons participate in the hard scattering at leading order. This is the program of the
STAR and PHENIX experiments at the RHIC polarized proton collider. The idea is to measure
the longitudinal double-spin asymmetry in high-transverse-momentum jet (or pion)
production,
\begin{equation}
    A_{LL} \;=\; \frac{\sigma^{++} - \sigma^{+-}}{\sigma^{++} + \sigma^{+-}},
    \label{eq:ALL-rhic}
\end{equation}
where the superscripts label the helicities of the two colliding protons. At the high
energies of RHIC, jet production is dominated by the subprocesses $gg\to gg$ and
$qg\to qg$, both of which involve at least one gluon, so $A_{LL}$ is directly sensitive
to $\Delta g$; other channels such as $qg\to q\gamma$ provide complementary constraints
with different systematics. The measured asymmetries are small but significantly
positive, establishing that $\Delta g$ is non-zero and positive over the measured range
of gluon momentum fraction.

Assembling all the measured pieces, the sum rule reads approximately
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\,\Delta\Sigma + \Delta G + L_{q} + L_{g}
    \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2}\times 0.3 + (0.2\text{--}0.4) + L_{q} + L_{g},
    \label{eq:spin-sum-numbers}
\end{equation}
with the quark helicity contributing about $0.3$ and the gluon helicity, integrated over
the measured range, about $0.2$--$0.4$. The quark and gluon spins together do not saturate
the sum rule; the balance must be supplied by the orbital angular momenta $L_{q}$ and
$L_{g}$, which remain the least well determined ingredients and are the primary
motivation for the multidimensional structure studies of the following subsections.
Lattice QCD offers an independent, first-principles route to the same decomposition, and
the nucleon spin structure has been among its targets from early on
\cite[Ch.~6]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{Beyond collinear PDFs: transverse-momentum-dependent distributions}
\label{subsec:tmds}

Everything so far---the momentum distributions $q(x)$, $\bar q(x)$, $g(x)$ and their
helicity analogues $\Delta q(x)$, $\Delta\bar q(x)$, $\Delta g(x)$---describes only the
\emph{longitudinal} motion of the partons, their share of the nucleon momentum along the
direction of the fast-moving nucleon. This one-dimensional picture cannot address the
orbital angular momentum $L_{q}$, $L_{g}$ left undetermined by the spin sum rule, because
orbital motion is transverse motion, and it is invisible to a purely collinear
description. A complete description must allow the parton a \emph{transverse} momentum,
promoting the distribution from $f(x)$ to $f(x,\vec k_{T})$: a probability density to
find a parton with longitudinal momentum fraction $x$ \emph{and} transverse momentum
$\vec k_{T}$. This is a three-dimensional momentum-space picture, or momentum-space
tomography, of the nucleon, and it generalizes the single collinear PDF into a family of
\emph{transverse-momentum-dependent} distributions (TMDs).

The experimental key is to detect one final-state hadron in addition to the scattered
lepton, so that its transverse momentum records the transverse motion of the struck
parton. This is semi-inclusive deep inelastic scattering (SIDIS),
\begin{equation}
    eN \;\rightarrow\; e' h X,
\end{equation}
in which $e$ and $e'$ define the hard scattering exactly as in inclusive DIS, while the
observed hadron $h$ carries information about the struck parton and how it fragments into
hadrons. At leading order the cross section factorizes into a hard partonic cross section,
a TMD parton distribution and a fragmentation function,
\begin{equation}
    E'E_{h}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{ep\to e'hX}}{\mathrm{d}^{3}l'\,\mathrm{d}^{3}P_{h}}
    \;\approx\; \hat\sigma_{eq\to e'q'}\otimes f_{1}(x,\vec k_{T})
    \,\widetilde{\otimes}\, D_{h/q'}(z,\vec k_{T}'),
    \label{eq:sidis-factorization}
\end{equation}
where the momentum fraction carried by the observed hadron is
\begin{equation}
    z \;=\; \frac{P\cdot P_{h}}{P\cdot q}.
    \label{eq:z-def}
\end{equation}
The new non-perturbative object is the \emph{fragmentation function}
$D_{h/q'}(z,\vec k_{T}')$, the probability density for a quark of flavor $q'$ to fragment
into a hadron $h$ carrying a fraction $z$ of its momentum. Its necessity is a direct
consequence of confinement: the struck quark can never be observed directly but must
hadronize into a final-state hadron. SIDIS therefore depends on two kinds of
non-perturbative information---the partonic structure of the target and the fragmentation
of the struck parton---and is correspondingly more differential than inclusive DIS, at the
price of introducing the fragmentation functions.

Allowing transverse momentum and polarization multiplies the number of distributions. At
leading twist there are eight TMD parton distributions, classified by the polarization of
the nucleon (unpolarized $U$, longitudinal $L$, transverse $T$) and of the quark (again
$U$, $L$, $T$). Three survive the collinear limit and are the familiar PDFs: the
unpolarized $f_{1}$, the helicity $g_{1}$ and the transversity $h_{1}$. The remaining five
are genuinely new and encode spin--momentum correlations that vanish once $\vec k_{T}$ is
integrated out: the Sivers function $f_{1T}^{\perp}$ (an unpolarized quark in a
transversely polarized nucleon), the Boer--Mulders function $h_{1}^{\perp}$ (a transversely
polarized quark in an unpolarized nucleon), the two worm-gear functions $g_{1T}^{\perp}$
and $h_{1L}^{\perp}$, and the pretzelosity $h_{1T}^{\perp}$. A parallel classification
applies to the TMD fragmentation functions, of which the Collins function $H_{1}^{\perp}$
is the most important. Together these functions map the correlation between the spin of
the nucleon, the spin of the quark and the quark's transverse motion.

\subsection{Azimuthal asymmetries and the Collins effect}
\label{subsec:collins}

A defining feature of SIDIS is that, once a hadron is detected, the cross section depends
on the azimuthal angles of that hadron and of the target spin around the virtual-photon
direction. Two planes are defined---the lepton plane and the hadron plane---and the
cross section is modulated by terms such as $\cos\phi_{h}$, $\cos 2\phi_{h}$,
$\sin\phi_{h}$ and $\sin(\phi_{h}-\phi_{S})$, where $\phi_{h}$ is the azimuth of the
produced hadron and $\phi_{S}$ that of the target spin. Each modulation isolates a
different combination of a TMD and a fragmentation function, so measuring the azimuthal
dependence at various beam and target polarizations decomposes the full structure into
its individual TMDs \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}.

The complete leading-twist SIDIS cross section is a sum of structure functions, each
carrying a characteristic azimuthal modulation and a definite beam and target
polarization dependence; a convenient reference is the TMD Handbook (JLAB-THY-23-3780).
Its overall structure,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{6}\sigma}
         {\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{S}\,
          \mathrm{d}\phi_{h}\,\mathrm{d}P_{hT}^{2}}
    \;\propto\; \frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{em}}^{2}}{x y Q^{2}}
      \left(1 - y + \tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}\right)
      \Bigl[F_{UU,T} + \cos(2\phi_{h})\,p_{1}F_{UU}^{\cos 2\phi_{h}}
      + \cdots\Bigr],
    \label{eq:full-sidis}
\end{equation}
groups the structure functions into unpolarized ($F_{UU}$), polarized-target
($F_{UL},F_{UT}$), and polarized-beam-and-target ($F_{LL},F_{LT}$) contributions, with
the subscripts labeling the beam and target polarizations. Each structure function is a
convolution, denoted $\mathcal{C}[\cdots]$, of a TMD distribution with a fragmentation
function; for example $F_{UU,T}=\mathcal{C}[f_{1}D_{1}]$ and
$F_{LL}=\mathcal{C}[g_{1}D_{1}]$.

The best-known modulation is the Collins effect. The Collins fragmentation function
$H_{1}^{\perp}$ measures the correlation between the transverse spin of the fragmenting
quark and the transverse momentum of the produced hadron; because it multiplies the
transversity distribution $h_{1}(x,\vec k_{T})$ in the cross section, it opens the only
practical path to measuring $h_{1}$, which---being chiral-odd---cannot appear alone in
inclusive DIS. The relevant single-spin asymmetry on a transversely polarized target is
\begin{equation}
    A_{UT}^{\sin(\phi_{h}+\phi_{S})}
    = \frac{F_{UT}^{\sin(\phi_{h}+\phi_{S})}}{F_{UU,T}}
    \;\propto\; \frac{h_{1}\,H_{1}^{\perp}}{f_{1}\,D_{1}},
    \label{eq:collins-asymmetry}
\end{equation}
in which the chiral-odd transversity $h_{1}$ is paired with the chiral-odd Collins
function $H_{1}^{\perp}$ so that the product is chiral-even and observable. Measurements at
HERMES and COMPASS have established non-zero Collins asymmetries for charged pions of
opposite sign for $\pi^{+}$ and $\pi^{-}$, and global analyses of these data now provide
extractions of the up- and down-quark transversity and of the Collins and Sivers
functions.

\subsection{The Drell--Yan process: sea-quark asymmetry and TMD universality}
\label{subsec:drell-yan}

SIDIS probes TMDs by scattering a lepton off a quark and detecting the fragmented hadron.
The Drell--Yan process probes the same distributions from the opposite direction: instead
of scattering a lepton beam off the nucleon, one lets a quark and an antiquark from two
colliding hadrons annihilate into a virtual photon that materializes as a lepton pair,
\begin{equation}
    q\bar q \;\rightarrow\; \gamma^{*} \;\rightarrow\; l^{+}l^{-},
    \qquad\text{or, at hadron level,}\qquad
    h_{1}h_{2} \;\rightarrow\; l^{+}l^{-}\,X.
    \label{eq:drell-yan}
\end{equation}
The final-state leptons do not interact strongly, so the measurement is theoretically
clean; the price is that both particles in the initial state are hadrons, so the process
probes a product of two parton distributions rather than a distribution convoluted with a
fragmentation function. Drell--Yan gives access to antiquarks and the sea in a way
complementary to SIDIS, and, as discussed below, it allows the \emph{process dependence}
of the TMDs to be tested directly.

A first application is the flavor asymmetry of the light-quark sea. Consider
$p+p\to\mu\mu+X$ and $p+n\to\mu\mu+X$, using isospin symmetry to relate the neutron and
proton distributions ($u_{p}=d_{n}$, $d_{p}=u_{n}$, and likewise for the antiquarks,
which follows from the $I=\tfrac{1}{2}$ doublet structure of the nucleon). At leading
order the cross section is a charge-weighted product of quark and antiquark densities,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}}
    \;\propto\; \sum_{q} e_{q}^{2}\,
    \bigl[q(x_{1})\bar q(x_{2}) + \bar q(x_{1})q(x_{2})\bigr].
    \label{eq:dy-xsec}
\end{equation}
Choosing kinematics with $x_{1}\gg x_{2}$, so that a valence quark from the first proton
annihilates a sea antiquark from the second, the cross sections simplify to
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{pp} &\propto 4u(x_{1})\bar u(x_{2}) + d(x_{1})\bar d(x_{2}),
    \label{eq:sigma-pp}\\
    \sigma_{pn} &\propto 4u(x_{1})\bar d(x_{2}) + d(x_{1})\bar u(x_{2}),
    \label{eq:sigma-pn}
\end{align}
and their ratio is directly sensitive to the sea-quark flavor asymmetry,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sigma_{pn}}{\sigma_{pp}} \;\approx\; \frac{\bar d(x_{2})}{\bar u(x_{2})}.
    \label{eq:dbar-ubar}
\end{equation}
The SeaQuest experiment at Fermilab measured exactly this ratio, finding $\bar d>\bar u$
over a broad range of $x$. The same asymmetry shows up in inclusive DIS as a violation of
the Gottfried sum rule, whose naive value presupposes a flavor-symmetric sea; the two
observations are different views of one fact \cite[Ch.~4.4]{ThomasWeise2001}. A natural
explanation is the pion cloud: the proton fluctuates
into a neutron plus a $\pi^{+}$,
\begin{equation}
    p(uud) \;\rightarrow\; n(udd) + \pi^{+}(u\bar d),
    \label{eq:pion-cloud-fluctuation}
\end{equation}
and the $\bar d$ in the $\pi^{+}$ enhances the down antiquark sea relative to the up
\cite[Ch.~7]{ThomasWeise2001}. The
same fluctuation appeared in the neutron-structure discussion of
Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}, and it will return in
Sect.~\ref{subsec:meson-structure} as the mechanism that makes the pion itself
experimentally accessible.

The Drell--Yan process accesses the same TMDs as SIDIS, but through annihilation rather
than scattering, so the transverse momentum and angular distribution of the lepton pair
reflect the transverse momenta of the annihilating partons. This makes possible one of
the sharpest predictions of TMD factorization: the \emph{Sivers sign change}. The Sivers
function $f_{1T}^{\perp}(x,\vec k_{T})$ describes an unpolarized quark inside a
transversely polarized nucleon, and it arises from the color interaction of the struck
quark with the remnant---a final-state interaction in SIDIS but an initial-state
interaction in Drell--Yan. While the ordinary spin-averaged TMD is process independent,
\begin{equation}
    f_{1}^{\mathrm{SIDIS}}(x,\vec k_{T}) = f_{1}^{\mathrm{DY}}(x,\vec k_{T}),
    \label{eq:f1-universal}
\end{equation}
the Sivers function is predicted to reverse sign between the two processes,
\begin{equation}
    f_{1T}^{\mathrm{SIDIS}}(x,\vec k_{T}) = -\,f_{1T}^{\mathrm{DY}}(x,\vec k_{T}),
    \label{eq:sivers-sign-change}
\end{equation}
a direct consequence of the different gauge-link structure in the two cases. Verifying
this sign change is a central goal of the current experimental program and a fundamental
test of the gauge structure of QCD.

The determination of TMDs is a worldwide effort combining three complementary processes:
SIDIS (at HERMES, COMPASS, Jefferson Lab and the future Electron--Ion Collider),
Drell--Yan (at Fermilab, RHIC, J-PARC and elsewhere), and $e^{+}e^{-}$ annihilation into
hadron pairs (at Belle, BaBar and BESIII), the last providing the cleanest access to the
fragmentation functions that enter the other two.

\subsection{Exclusive reactions and generalized parton distributions}
\label{subsec:gpds}

Even the TMDs do not complete the picture. The collinear PDFs give the longitudinal
momentum structure $f(x)$, and the TMDs extend this to the transverse momentum
$f(x,\vec k_{T})$, but both are distributions in \emph{momentum} space. Neither says
where inside the nucleon a parton of given momentum fraction is actually located. The
spatial distribution of partons is a separate question, and answering it requires a
different class of measurement \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}, \cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The route to spatial information is already familiar from elastic scattering. There the
form factor is the Fourier transform of a spatial charge distribution
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:form-factors}), so that resolving position required measuring the
dependence on the momentum transferred to the \emph{target as a whole}. Inclusive DIS
destroys precisely this information, because the target is shattered and the momentum
transfer to it is never reconstructed. What is needed is a reaction that resolves an
individual parton, as DIS does, while still leaving the nucleon intact and measuring the
momentum transferred to it, as elastic scattering does. These are the \emph{exclusive}
reactions, in which every final-state particle is detected---experimentally demanding,
but rewarded with a variable that is the Fourier conjugate of transverse position while
the struck quark remains identified.

The two workhorse channels are deeply virtual Compton scattering (DVCS),
\begin{equation}
    eN \;\rightarrow\; eN\gamma,
    \label{eq:dvcs-reaction}
\end{equation}
in which the virtual photon is absorbed and a real photon emitted by the same quark, and
deeply virtual meson production (DVMP),
\begin{equation}
    eN \;\rightarrow\; eN M,
    \label{eq:dvmp-reaction}
\end{equation}
in which a meson $M=\pi,\rho,\omega,\dots$ is produced instead and the recoiling baryon
may also be a hyperon. In both cases the amplitude factorizes into a hard part, governed
by QED and perturbative QCD and therefore calculable, and a soft part that is
non-perturbative and must be parametrized. The functions that parametrize the soft part
are the \emph{generalized parton distributions} (GPDs), which appear in the older
literature---and in Thomas and Weise---under the name \emph{off-forward parton
distributions} \cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A GPD depends on three variables rather than one. The first, $x$, is the average
longitudinal momentum fraction of the struck parton, which enters the amplitude with
fraction $x+\xi$ and leaves it with $x-\xi$. The second is the \emph{skewness} $\xi$, the
longitudinal momentum transfer, related to the Bjorken variable by
\begin{equation}
    \xi \;\approx\; \frac{x_{B}}{2 - x_{B}}.
    \label{eq:skewness}
\end{equation}
The third is the invariant momentum transfer to the hadron,
\begin{equation}
    t \;=\; (P' - P)^{2},
    \label{eq:mandelstam-t}
\end{equation}
which is the variable conjugate to transverse position. Counting the possible
helicity structures at leading twist gives eight GPDs. Four of them, $H$, $E$,
$\widetilde H$ and $\widetilde E$, describe processes in which the helicity of the struck
quark is conserved, and four, $H_{T}$, $E_{T}$, $\widetilde H_{T}$ and $\widetilde
E_{T}$, describe processes in which it is flipped; the helicity itself is defined as in
Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}, as the projection of the spin onto the direction
of motion.

What makes the GPDs conceptually central is that they contain the form factors and the
parton distributions as limiting cases, unifying the two structure descriptions that
have run in parallel through these two chapters. In the forward limit, in which no
momentum is transferred to the nucleon ($t\to 0$, $\xi\to 0$), the helicity-conserving
GPDs reduce to the ordinary parton distributions,
\begin{align}
    H^{q}(x,0,0) &= q(x),
    \label{eq:gpd-forward-H}\\
    \widetilde H^{q}(x,0,0) &= \Delta q(x),
    \label{eq:gpd-forward-Htilde}
\end{align}
recovering the unpolarized and helicity distributions of
Sects.~\ref{subsec:parton-model} and~\ref{subsec:polarized-dis}. Integrating instead over
the momentum fraction $x$ removes the parton-level information and leaves the elastic
form factors of Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering},
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-1}^{1} H^{q}(x,\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}x = F_{1}^{q}(t),
    \qquad
    \int_{-1}^{1} E^{q}(x,\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}x = F_{2}^{q}(t).
    \label{eq:gpd-form-factors}
\end{equation}
A single family of functions therefore interpolates between the spatial picture of
elastic scattering and the momentum picture of DIS, and contains correlations---between
where a parton sits and what momentum fraction it carries---that neither limit retains
\cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{Deeply virtual Compton scattering}
\label{subsec:dvcs}

DVCS is the deep inelastic limit of the virtual Compton scattering process already met at
low energies in connection with the nucleon polarizabilities
\cite[Ch.~2]{ThomasWeise2001}. It is the golden channel for extracting GPDs, because it
is the exclusive reaction with the cleanest factorization: the hard subprocess is pure QED
Compton scattering off a
quark, with no additional non-perturbative object beyond the GPDs themselves. The price
is that it is sensitive only to the four helicity-conserving GPDs $H$, $E$, $\widetilde H$
and $\widetilde E$; the helicity-flip distributions require the meson channel of the next
subsection.

Factorization in DVCS holds at the level of the \emph{amplitude} rather than the cross
section, which makes the connection between data and distributions a two-step process.
The measured observables first determine a set of \emph{Compton form factors} (CFFs)
$\mathcal{H}$, $\mathcal{E}$, $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}$,
which are convolutions of the GPDs with the hard kernel and therefore access them only in
integrated form; the CFFs must then be deconvoluted to recover the GPDs themselves. This
second step is the harder one and remains a major source of model dependence.

A further complication is experimental. The final state $eN\gamma$ can be reached not
only through DVCS but also through the Bethe--Heitler process, in which the real photon is
radiated by the electron rather than by the nucleon, with the nucleon structure entering
only through the ordinary elastic form factors. The two are indistinguishable, so the
amplitudes add coherently,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A} \;=\; \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}} + \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{DVCS}},
    \label{eq:bh-dvcs-amplitude}
\end{equation}
and the cross section contains the squares of both together with their interference,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}\sigma \;\propto\; |\mathcal{A}|^{2}
    = \sum\bigl|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}} + \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{DVCS}}\bigr|^{2}
    = \sum \Bigl[\,|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}}|^{2} + |\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{DVCS}}|^{2}
      + \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}}^{*}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{DVCS}}
      + \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{DVCS}}^{*}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}}\,\Bigr].
    \label{eq:bh-interference}
\end{equation}
At typical kinematics the Bethe--Heitler contribution dominates, which at first sight
looks fatal. It is in fact an advantage: because $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{BH}}$ is large and
exactly calculable from the known elastic form factors, the interference terms act as an
amplifier, and the small DVCS amplitude can be extracted linearly from the interference
rather than quadratically from its own square. This is the same strategy of using a large
known reference amplitude to measure a small unknown one that underlies homodyne
detection in optics, and the interference of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bh-interference} was
recognized as the practical route to DVCS as soon as off-forward distributions were
proposed \cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The observables of choice are again asymmetries, labeled by the beam and target
polarizations. The beam-spin asymmetry, formed from the counts $N^{+}$ and $N^{-}$ with
the two beam helicities,
\begin{equation}
    A_{LU} \;=\; \frac{N^{+} - N^{-}}{N^{+} + N^{-}},
    \label{eq:beam-spin-asymmetry}
\end{equation}
is measured as a function of the angle $\phi$ between the leptonic and hadronic planes,
and different polarization combinations project out different CFFs: schematically
$A_{LU}\sim\mathrm{Im}\{\mathcal{H},\widetilde{\mathcal{H}},\mathcal{E}\}$,
$A_{UL}\sim\mathrm{Im}\{\mathcal{H},\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\}$,
$A_{LL}\sim\mathrm{Re}\{\mathcal{H},\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\}$ and
$A_{UT}\sim\mathrm{Im}\{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{E}\}$, where the first subscript refers to
the beam and the second to the target. Because the asymmetries constrain the real and
imaginary parts of different combinations, a full separation requires many polarization
configurations combined with differential cross-section measurements. Measurements of the
DVCS beam-spin asymmetry over a range of $x_{B}$, $Q^{2}$ and $t$ show the characteristic
$\sin\phi$ modulation predicted by the interference and are well described by the leading
GPD models.

\subsection{Deeply virtual meson production}
\label{subsec:dvmp}

Producing a meson instead of a photon, $eN\to eNM$, provides an alternative and
complementary route to the GPDs. Its principal advantage is variety: many different
mesons can be produced, and because each has its own flavor content and quantum numbers,
the set of measurements filters different flavor and helicity combinations. In
particular, and unlike DVCS, meson production gives access to the helicity-flip GPDs
$H_{T}$, $E_{T}$, $\widetilde H_{T}$ and $\widetilde E_{T}$.

The disadvantages are equally clear. Factorization is not as firmly established as in
DVCS, and the amplitude contains an additional non-perturbative object, the
\emph{distribution amplitude} of the produced meson, which describes how the quark pair
converts into the observed hadron and must itself be modeled or measured. The extraction
is therefore again multi-step: measured asymmetries yield helicity amplitudes, which in
turn are interpreted in terms of GPDs. The compensation is that the same data also carry
information about the internal structure of the produced meson, a theme taken up in
Sect.~\ref{subsec:meson-structure}.

CLAS12 has begun a broad DVMP program based on beam-spin asymmetries of the form
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BSA}
    = \frac{\sqrt{2\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}\;\dfrac{\sigma_{LT'}}{\sigma_{0}}\,\sin\phi}
           {1 + \sqrt{2\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\;\dfrac{\sigma_{LT}}{\sigma_{0}}\cos\phi
            + \epsilon\,\dfrac{\sigma_{TT}}{\sigma_{0}}\cos 2\phi},
    \label{eq:dvmp-bsa}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is the virtual-photon polarization parameter and the $\sigma_{ij}$ are
the interference and polarization structure functions of the photoproduction cross
section. The numerator carries the physics: the longitudinal--transverse interference
term is sensitive to the helicity-flip distributions through
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{LT'} \;\sim\;
    \mathrm{Im}\Bigl[\,\langle\bar E_{T}\rangle^{*}\langle\widetilde H\rangle
    + \langle H_{T}\rangle^{*}\langle\widetilde E\rangle\,\Bigr],
    \qquad
    \bar E_{T} = 2\widetilde H_{T} + E_{T},
    \label{eq:sigma-ltprime}
\end{equation}
so that a measurement of the $\sin\phi$ modulation constrains combinations of GPDs that
DVCS cannot reach at all.

\subsection{Nucleon tomography and the Ji sum rule}
\label{subsec:tomography}

The reward for the experimental difficulty of exclusive reactions is a genuine spatial
image of the nucleon. At zero skewness the variable $t$ reduces to the square of a purely
transverse momentum transfer, $t=-\Delta^{2}$, and the Fourier transform of the GPD $H$
with respect to $\vec\Delta$ yields a density in the transverse plane,
\begin{equation}
    q(x,\vec b_{\perp}) \;=\; \int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Delta}{4\pi^{2}}\;
    \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec b_{\perp}\cdot\vec\Delta}\;
    H^{q}(x,0,t=-\Delta^{2}),
    \label{eq:impact-parameter}
\end{equation}
where $\vec b_{\perp}$ is the impact parameter, the transverse distance of the parton from
the center of momentum of the nucleon. This is the probability density to find a quark
carrying longitudinal momentum fraction $x$ at transverse position $\vec b_{\perp}$: a
three-dimensional picture combining one momentum and two spatial coordinates, and the
direct analogue in the parton regime of the charge distribution extracted from the elastic
form factors in Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering}. Mapping it out as a function of $x$
is \emph{nucleon tomography}, and it reveals how the transverse size of the parton cloud
shrinks as $x$ increases, the fast partons sitting near the center while the slow sea
spreads out to the periphery.

GPDs also offer a route to the missing piece of the spin sum rule. The Ji sum rule states
that the total angular momentum---spin plus orbital---carried by quarks of flavor $q$ is
the second moment of the sum of two GPDs in the forward limit,
\begin{equation}
    J^{q} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\int x\,\bigl[H^{q}(x,\xi,0) + E^{q}(x,\xi,0)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x,
    \label{eq:ji-sum-rule}
\end{equation}
the result being independent of $\xi$ \cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}. This is a striking
statement: a quantity that
cannot be measured directly, the orbital angular momentum, becomes accessible through an
integral over a measurable distribution, since subtracting the separately known helicity
contribution $\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\Sigma$ from $J^{q}$ leaves $L_{q}$. It is, in principle,
the way to close the accounting of Eq.~\eqref{eq:spin-sum-numbers} and settle the proton
spin crisis. In practice the sum rule requires knowledge of $H$ and $E$ over the full
range of $x$, including regions that no experiment constrains, and the GPD $E$ in
particular is poorly known. The measurements so far are not sufficient to determine
$J^{q}$ with useful precision, and doing so remains one of the principal goals of the
field.

\subsection{Timelike form factors and timelike Compton scattering}
\label{subsec:timelike}

All the reactions discussed so far involve a spacelike virtual photon, $Q^{2}=-q^{2}>0$.
The crossed kinematic region, in which the photon is timelike ($q^{2}>0$, that is
$Q^{2}<0$), is reached by \emph{creating} a hadron--antihadron pair out of a virtual
photon rather than scattering off an existing hadron, as in
\begin{equation}
    e^{+}e^{-} \;\rightarrow\; \gamma^{*} \;\rightarrow\;
    p\bar p,\;\pi^{+}\pi^{-},\;K^{+}K^{-}.
    \label{eq:timelike-annihilation}
\end{equation}
The form factors measured this way are the analytic continuations of the spacelike ones
and are related to them by crossing symmetry, so timelike measurements constrain the same
underlying structure from a region that elastic scattering cannot reach. They are
particularly valuable for unstable hadrons, for which no target exists, and they were
already encountered in Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering} in the form of transition
form factors measured in the radiative decays of hyperons and mesons. The analytic
continuation between the two regions, and the role of the vector mesons in the spectral
functions that connect them, are treated in detail by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The same crossing applies to the exclusive reactions of the previous subsections.
Timelike Compton scattering, the process in which a real photon converts on the nucleon
into a lepton pair, is the time-reversed analogue of DVCS and is described by the same
GPDs. Measuring both and comparing them is a non-trivial test of the universality of the
distributions and of the analytic structure of the amplitudes, in the same spirit as the
Sivers sign-change test for TMDs.

\subsection{The structure of mesons}
\label{subsec:meson-structure}

Everything in this chapter has concerned the nucleon, for the practical reason that
protons and neutrons are the only hadrons available as targets. But nothing in the
formalism is specific to the nucleon: parton distributions, form factors, radii and
generalized distributions are defined for every hadron, and the pion in particular is of
special interest. As the Goldstone boson of spontaneously broken chiral symmetry it is
anomalously light, and understanding how a nearly massless bound state distributes its
momentum among quarks and gluons is a sharp test of QCD.

The obstacle is that no stable meson target exists, so the structure of mesons must be
reached indirectly. One route reverses the roles of beam and target and uses a meson beam
on a nucleon target in the Drell--Yan process. At lowest order the dimuon cross section
factorizes into a hard kernel and one distribution from each colliding hadron,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}M_{ll}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y}
    = \frac{4\pi\alpha_{\mathrm{em}}^{2}}{9M_{ll}^{2}s}\sum_{ij}
      \int_{x_{\pi}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{\pi}}{x_{\pi}}
      \int_{x_{N}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{N}}{x_{N}}\;
      C_{ij}\!\left(x_{\pi},x_{N},M_{ll}/\mu\right)
      f_{i}^{\pi}(x_{\pi},\mu)\,f_{j}^{N}(x_{N},\mu),
    \label{eq:pion-dy}
\end{equation}
where $C_{ij}$ is the hard scattering kernel, $f_{i}^{\pi}$ the pion parton distribution
for flavor $i$, $f_{j}^{N}$ the nucleon parton distribution and $\mu$ the renormalization
scale. Since the nucleon distributions are known from the global fits of
Sect.~\ref{subsec:dglap}, the pion distributions can be extracted. Separating valence from
sea requires the usual symmetry arguments. The valence content of the charged pions is
\begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{+}} = u^{\pi^{+}} - \bar u^{\pi^{+}},
    \qquad
    d_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{-}} = d^{\pi^{-}} - \bar d^{\pi^{-}},
    \label{eq:pion-valence}
\end{equation}
isospin symmetry relates the two charge states through
\begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{+}} = \bar d_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{+}}
    = \bar u_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{-}} = d_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi^{-}},
    \label{eq:pion-isospin}
\end{equation}
and assuming SU(3) symmetry makes the sea flavor blind,
$\bar u_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}=u_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}=\bar d_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}
=d_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}=\bar s_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}=s_{\mathrm{sea}}^{\pi}$. With an
isoscalar (deuteron) target the valence distribution is then isolated by suitable
combinations of the $\pi^{+}$ and $\pi^{-}$ induced cross sections,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi\mathrm{D}} = \sigma^{\pi^{-}D} - \sigma^{\pi^{+}D},
    \qquad
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{val}}^{\pi\mathrm{D}} = 4\sigma^{\pi^{+}D} - \sigma^{\pi^{-}D}.
    \label{eq:pion-valence-combinations}
\end{equation}

A second and rather elegant route exploits the pion cloud that has already appeared twice
in this chapter. The physical proton is a superposition of a bare three-quark state and
virtual meson--baryon configurations,
\begin{equation}
    \ket{p} = a\ket{uud} + b\ket{n\pi^{+}} + c\ket{p\pi^{0}}
    + d\ket{\Delta^{++}\pi^{-}} + e\ket{\Lambda K^{+}}
    + f\ket{\Sigma^{0}K^{+}} + g\ket{\Sigma^{+}K^{0}} + \cdots,
    \label{eq:pion-cloud-expansion}
\end{equation}
whose meson--baryon components are controlled, at least in their long-range part, by
chiral symmetry \cite[Ch.~7]{ThomasWeise2001}. One may then ask whether the virtual meson
in such a configuration can itself be used as a target. It can: in the \emph{Sullivan
process} the electron scatters off the virtual pion
or kaon while the recoiling baryon is detected at small momentum transfer, and the cross
section factorizes into a flux of virtual mesons and the cross section on the meson,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\sigma(ep\to e'nX)}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{L}}
    = f_{\pi/p}(x_{L},t)\times\sigma(e\pi\to e'X),
    \label{eq:sullivan}
\end{equation}
where $x_{L}$ is the longitudinal momentum fraction carried by the recoiling baryon. This
gives access to the pion structure functions and form factor, at the cost of the pion flux
parametrization $f_{\pi/p}$, whose model dependence is the dominant uncertainty. Combining
Drell--Yan data, leading-neutron (Sullivan) data and transverse-momentum spectra, global
analyses now determine the pion valence, sea and gluon distributions, with the gluon
carrying a substantial momentum fraction as in the nucleon.

The electromagnetic radii of the mesons are measured by yet another inversion of the usual
setup. Since no meson target exists, one uses a meson \emph{beam} on a stable lepton
target, measuring $\pi/K + e^{-}\to\pi/K + e^{-}$ in inverse kinematics. Beyond
necessity, this arrangement has a real advantage: with the electron initially at rest
there is no incoming external bremsstrahlung, so an important class of radiative
corrections is absent. The kinematics are, however, restrictive. With the electron mass
$m_{e}$, beam energy $E_{b}$ and beam momentum $p_{b}$,
\begin{equation}
    Q^{2} \approx 2m_{e}E_{e},
    \qquad
    s = 2E_{b}m_{e} + m_{b}^{2} + m_{e}^{2},
    \qquad
    Q^{2}_{\max} = \frac{4m_{e}^{2}p_{b}^{2}}{s} = 4p_{\mathrm{cm}}^{2},
    \label{eq:inverse-kinematics}
\end{equation}
and the small electron mass caps the accessible momentum transfer severely: a
$190~\mathrm{GeV}$ beam reaches only $Q^{2}_{\max}\approx 0.176~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$ for
pions and $0.086~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$ for kaons, so that high beam energies are essential.

The classic measurements were made by the NA7 experiment at CERN. With a
$300~\mathrm{GeV}$ pion beam and some $380{,}000$ events, the pion charge radius came out
as
\begin{equation}
    \langle r^{2}\rangle_{\pi} = 0.439 \pm 0.008~\mathrm{fm}^{2},
    \qquad
    \sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{\pi}} = 0.663~\mathrm{fm},
    \label{eq:pion-radius}
\end{equation}
extracted from the slope of the form factor at the origin exactly as for the proton in
Chapter~\ref{sec:electron-scattering} (Sect.~\ref{subsec:proton-radius}), a definition
whose subtleties are discussed by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2]{ThomasWeise2001}. The kaon
measurement, with a $250~\mathrm{GeV}$ beam and only some $30{,}000$ events, gave
\begin{equation}
    \langle r^{2}\rangle_{K} = 0.34 \pm 0.05~\mathrm{fm}^{2},
    \qquad
    \sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{K}} = 0.58~\mathrm{fm}.
    \label{eq:kaon-radius}
\end{equation}
Both quoted uncertainties are remarkably small, and both deserve scepticism of the kind
that the proton radius puzzle taught: the errors are statistical and do not reflect the
model dependence of the extrapolation to $Q^{2}=0$, even though the original publications
asserted insensitivity to the assumed functional form of the form factor. Given how much
the analogous extraction for the proton has moved since, remeasuring the pion and kaon
radii with modern statistics and a transparent treatment of the model uncertainty is an
obvious and overdue task.

\subsection{The unified picture: generalized TMDs and outlook}
\label{subsec:gtmds}

The distributions introduced in this chapter and the last are not independent objects but
projections of a single, more general one. The \emph{generalized transverse-momentum
dependent distributions} (GTMDs), functions of the momentum fraction $x$, the parton
transverse momentum $\vec k_{\perp}$ and the momentum transfer $\vec\Delta$, contain all
of the information carried by the others. Setting $\Delta=0$ removes the momentum
transfer and yields the TMDs; integrating over $\vec k_{\perp}$ yields the GPDs;
integrating over $x$ yields transverse-momentum-dependent form factors; and successive
projections lead down to the ordinary PDFs, the elastic form factors and, at the bottom of
the hierarchy, the total charge. Figure~\ref{fig:gtmd-diamond} shows the resulting
lattice of relations, sometimes called the structure-function diamond, with the GTMD as
the mother distribution at its apex.

The GTMDs are, at present, largely a theoretical construct: it is not clear how to access
them experimentally, though double DVCS and two-jet production have been proposed as
possible channels. Their value in the meantime is organizational, since they make explicit
that the many distributions introduced here are different projections of one underlying
object and must satisfy relations among themselves.

It is worth closing by collecting the pieces. Table~\ref{tab:structure-summary}
summarizes the hierarchy: which reaction gives access to each class of distribution, on
what variables it depends, and what it tells us. Elastic scattering
($eN\to eN$) yields the Sachs form factors $G_{E}(Q^{2})$ and $G_{M}(Q^{2})$ and hence the
electromagnetic shape of the nucleon at resolution $Q^{2}$, including the charge radius
extracted from the slope $r_{p}^{2}=-6\,\mathrm{d}G_{E}/\mathrm{d}Q^{2}|_{Q^{2}=0}$.
Resonance electroproduction ($eN\to eN^{*}\to e+\mathrm{hadrons}$) yields the transition
form factors $G_{M}^{*}$, $G_{E}^{*}$ and $G_{C}^{*}$, measured through the helicity
amplitudes $A_{1/2}$, $A_{3/2}$ and $S_{1/2}$, and tells us how the nucleon is excited.
Inclusive DIS, polarized and unpolarized, yields the collinear distributions through
$F_{2}$ and $g_{1}$, with the DGLAP equations governing their evolution and global
analysis becoming indispensable from this point on. Semi-inclusive DIS and Drell--Yan
yield the TMDs and, in the SIDIS case, the fragmentation functions. Exclusive reactions
yield the GPDs and with them the transverse spatial distribution of partons. And all of
these, finally, exist for every hadron, not only for the nucleon---which is why the
pion and kaon of Sect.~\ref{subsec:meson-structure} belong in the same framework.

Many questions remain open, and answering them needs machines that do not yet exist. The
requirements are clear from what has been said: polarized beams of electrons, protons and
neutrons to form the spin asymmetries; a large-acceptance spectrometer to reconstruct the
complete final states that exclusive reactions demand; and high luminosity, because
exclusive cross sections are small and the interesting observables are differential in
several variables at once. These are precisely the design goals of the Electron--Ion
Collider under construction at Brookhaven, which is expected to map the gluon
distributions, settle the orbital angular momentum contribution to the proton spin
through the Ji sum rule, and extend nucleon tomography over a wide range in $x$ and
$Q^{2}$. The structure of hadrons, mapped in one dimension by the experiments of the
1970s, is only now becoming a genuinely multidimensional science. The pace is worth
noting: the standard monograph of Thomas and Weise could still treat the off-forward
distributions of Sect.~\ref{subsec:gpds} in a single closing section of its deep
inelastic chapter \cite[Ch.~4.8]{ThomasWeise2001}, whereas those
distributions, the TMDs and the GTMDs now between them define the program of a whole
generation of experiments.
