\section{Elastic and Resonant Probes of Nucleon Structure}
\label{sec:electron-scattering}

The spectroscopy of the preceding chapters mapped out \emph{which} hadrons exist and
how their masses and quantum numbers are organized, but it says nothing about what a
nucleon looks like \emph{inside}. A proton is not a point: it is an extended,
strongly bound system of quarks, antiquarks and gluons, and the questions that
spectroscopy cannot answer---how the electric charge and the magnetic moment are
distributed in space, how the constituents share the momentum and the spin of the
whole, and how those distributions change as we look at ever shorter distances---are
questions about \emph{structure}. This chapter and the next turn to the experimental
tool that has answered them most cleanly, the scattering of high-energy electrons off
nucleons. Here we treat the regime in which the target either recoils intact
(elastic scattering, encoded in the nucleon \emph{form factors}) or is excited to a
definite resonance (encoded in \emph{transition form factors}); the fully inelastic
regime, in which the nucleon is shattered and one measures the momentum distributions
of its partons, is the subject of Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure}. The standard
monograph on nucleon structure, Thomas and Weise \cite{ThomasWeise2001}, is used
throughout both chapters as the principal reference for the formalism; its Chapter~2,
on the electromagnetic structure of the nucleon, follows almost exactly the progression
from elastic scattering through the form factors to the nucleon resonances taken here.

\subsection{Motivation: probing nucleon structure with electron scattering}
\label{subsec:why-electron-scattering}

The idea of using a beam of light particles to reveal the structure of a target is as
old as subatomic physics itself. In the experiment that founded the field, Rutherford,
Geiger and Marsden scattered $\alpha$~particles off a thin gold foil and found a small
but unmistakable rate of large-angle deflections. For a pointlike, spinless projectile
of charge $ze$ scattering in the Coulomb field of a pointlike nucleus of charge $Ze$,
the differential cross section is the Rutherford formula
\begin{equation}
    \dv{\sigma}{\Omega}
    = \left(\frac{Z z e^{2}}{16\pi\epsilon_0 E}\right)^{\!2}
      \frac{1}{\sin^{4}\!\frac{\theta}{2}},
    \label{eq:rutherford}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the kinetic energy of the projectile and $\theta$ the scattering angle.
The steep $\sin^{-4}(\theta/2)$ rise toward small angles, and above all the survival of
the cross section out to large angles, were what forced the conclusion that the atomic
positive charge is concentrated in a tiny nucleus rather than spread through the atom
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}. The lesson is general: the angular distribution of a
scattered pointlike probe is a direct fingerprint of the charge distribution of the
target.

To resolve the interior of a nucleon rather than of an atom, one needs a probe that is
itself pointlike, whose interaction with the target is understood from first
principles, and that perturbs the target as little as possible. The electron satisfies
all three requirements. It is, as far as we can tell, a structureless Dirac particle,
so any structure seen in the cross section belongs to the target and not to the probe.
Its interaction is electromagnetic and therefore governed by quantum electrodynamics,
the best-tested theory in physics, so the ``ruler'' is calibrated exactly. And because
the electromagnetic coupling $\alpha \approx 1/137$ is small, a single virtual photon
is exchanged to very good approximation, so the reaction can be pictured as the clean
one-photon process of Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics} rather than as a complicated multiple
scattering. These are precisely the advantages that $\alpha$~particles lacked---an
$\alpha$ is composite and strongly interacting---and they are the reason
the modern study of nucleon structure is built on electron beams
\cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}. The electromagnetic current operator that makes this
programme quantitative, and the one-photon-exchange framework built on it, are set out
by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~1]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The price to pay is energy. A probe of momentum $q$ can resolve spatial structures no
smaller than of order its reduced wavelength, so that the accessible distance scale is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta x \;\sim\; \frac{\hbar c}{\sqrt{Q^{2}}},
    \qquad \hbar c \approx 0.197~\mathrm{GeV\,fm},
    \label{eq:resolving-power}
\end{equation}
where $Q^{2}=-q^{2}$ is the (positive) squared four-momentum transferred by the virtual
photon, defined precisely in the next subsection. Equation~\eqref{eq:resolving-power}
is the quantitative statement of why nucleon structure demands GeV-scale machines: to
probe the proton, whose radius is about $0.84~\mathrm{fm}$, requires
$Q^{2}\sim 0.05~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$, while resolving structures ten times smaller
requires $Q^{2}$ a hundred times larger. Table~\ref{tab:resolving-power} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:resolving-power} translate a few representative momentum transfers into
distances; the trend is monotonic and unforgiving, and it sets the scale of every
experiment discussed in this chapter and the next \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{Reaction kinematics and reaction types}
\label{subsec:kinematics}

The generic reaction underlying the whole chapter is the scattering of an electron off
a nucleon,
\begin{equation}
    e(k) + N(P) \;\rightarrow\; e(k') + X(P_X),
    \label{eq:generic-reaction}
\end{equation}
where $k$, $k'$, $P$ and $P_X$ are the four-momenta of the incoming electron, the
scattered electron, the target nucleon and the unobserved hadronic final state $X$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:kinematics}). In the one-photon-exchange approximation the electron
line couples to the nucleon through a single virtual photon that carries the
\emph{exchanged four-momentum}
\begin{equation}
    q \;=\; k - k',
\end{equation}
so that all of the information about the target is encoded in how it responds to a
photon of four-momentum $q$. The single most useful invariant built from $q$ is its
negative square,
\begin{equation}
    Q^{2} \;\equiv\; -q^{2} \;>\; 0,
\end{equation}
which is positive because the exchanged photon is spacelike; $Q^{2}$ is the virtuality
that already appeared in Eq.~\eqref{eq:resolving-power} and sets the resolution of the
probe.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Figures/ep_kinematics.png}
    \caption{One-photon-exchange kinematics of electron--nucleon scattering,
             Eq.~\eqref{eq:generic-reaction}. The incident electron
             $k=(E,\vec k)$ scatters to $k'=(E',\vec k')$, transferring a virtual
             photon of four-momentum $q=(\nu,\vec q)$ to a nucleon at rest
             $P=(M,\vec 0)$, which emerges as the hadronic system $X$. Adapted from the lecture slides (week 26, "Review: Reaction kinematics").}
    \label{fig:kinematics}
\end{figure}

Working in the rest frame of the target, $P=(M,\vec 0)$, the photon transfers to the
nucleon the \emph{energy}
\begin{equation}
    \nu \;=\; E - E' \;=\; \frac{P\cdot q}{M},
    \label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
the difference between the incoming and outgoing electron energies. Together $Q^{2}$
and $\nu$ specify the kinematics completely for an unpolarized measurement. Two
further invariants recur throughout: the squared invariant mass of the hadronic final
state,
\begin{equation}
    W^{2} \;=\; (P+q)^{2} \;=\; M^{2} + 2M\nu - Q^{2},
    \label{eq:W2}
\end{equation}
which distinguishes elastic scattering from resonance production and from the
continuum, and the dimensionless \emph{Bjorken variable}
\begin{equation}
    x_{B} \;=\; \frac{Q^{2}}{2P\cdot q} \;=\; \frac{Q^{2}}{2M\nu},
    \label{eq:xbj}
\end{equation}
whose meaning as the momentum fraction of the struck parton emerges in
Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure}. The elastic case is the special point at
which the target recoils intact, $W = M$, which by Eq.~\eqref{eq:W2} forces
$Q^{2}=2M\nu$ and hence $x_{B}=1$; every inelastic process has $W>M$ and $x_{B}<1$.

What distinguishes the several classes of measurement is not the elementary reaction
\eqref{eq:generic-reaction} but \emph{which} part of the final state is detected and
in which kinematic region. It is worth setting out the taxonomy at the start, because
each class probes a different structure quantity and the rest of this chapter, and the
next, is organized around it:
\begin{description}
    \item[Elastic scattering, $eN\to eN$.] The nucleon survives ($W=M$). The response
        is parametrized by two \emph{elastic form factors}, treated in
        Sects.~\ref{subsec:form-factors}--\ref{subsec:two-photon}.
    \item[Resonance electroproduction, $eN\to eN^{*}\to e+\text{hadrons}$.] The nucleon
        is excited to a definite resonance ($W\simeq M_{N^{*}}$). The response is
        parametrized by \emph{transition form factors}, treated in
        Sect.~\ref{subsec:transition-ff}.
    \item[Inclusive deep inelastic scattering (DIS), $eN\to eX$.] Only the scattered
        electron is detected and $W$ lies in the continuum. The response is
        parametrized by \emph{parton distribution functions}
        (Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure}).
    \item[Semi-inclusive DIS, $eN\to e\,h\,X$.] One final-state hadron $h$ is detected
        in addition to the electron, giving access to \emph{transverse-momentum
        dependent distributions} (Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure}).
    \item[Exclusive reactions, e.g.\ $eN\to eN\gamma$ or $eN\to eN\,M$.] The entire
        final state is reconstructed, giving access to \emph{generalized parton
        distributions} and meson form factors (beyond the scope of these notes).
\end{description}
The organizing principle is a ladder in $W$ and $Q^{2}$: at fixed low $W=M$ one measures
the ground-state form factors; stepping up in $W$ one reaches the resonances; and at
large $W$ and large $Q^{2}$ one enters the deep inelastic region where the photon
resolves individual partons. The present chapter climbs the first two rungs; the third
is the subject of Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure} \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}.
The invariants collected above, and the explicit cross-section formulae for lepton-- and
photon--nucleon scattering in each of these regimes, are given by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.1, App.~B]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{Mott scattering and elastic form factors}
\label{subsec:form-factors}

The Rutherford cross section \eqref{eq:rutherford} was derived for a spinless
projectile. An electron is a spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ Dirac particle, and accounting for its
spin modifies the angular distribution in a way that already contains physics. The
result is the \emph{Mott cross section}
\begin{equation}
    \left(\dv{\sigma}{\Omega}\right)_{\!\mathrm{Mott}}
    = \left(\dv{\sigma}{\Omega}\right)_{\!\mathrm{Rutherford}}
      \left(1 - \beta^{2}\sin^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)\frac{E'}{E},
    \qquad \beta = \frac{v}{c},
    \label{eq:mott}
\end{equation}
where the recoil factor $E'/E$ accounts for the energy carried away by a target of
finite mass. The characteristic suppression
$\left(1-\beta^{2}\sin^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$
at backward angles is a consequence of \emph{helicity conservation}. The helicity of a
relativistic particle,
\begin{equation}
    h = \frac{\vec s\cdot\vec p}{|\vec s|\,|\vec p|},
\end{equation}
the projection of the spin onto the direction of motion, is conserved for a
relativistic electron interacting through the vector coupling of QED. In the limit
$\beta\to 1$ the electron cannot reverse its momentum without flipping its helicity, so
backscattering off a spinless target is forbidden and the cross section vanishes at
$\theta=180^{\circ}$. Mott scattering is thus the correct pointlike baseline against
which the structure of a real, extended target is measured \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}.
The derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mott} from the Dirac current, together with the
helicity-conservation argument, is given in Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.1]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A real nucleon is not pointlike, and its finite extent shows up as a $Q^{2}$-dependent
reduction of the cross section below the Mott value. For a static charge distribution
$\rho(\vec r)$ the deviation is captured by a \emph{form factor}, the Fourier transform
of the spatial distribution,
\begin{equation}
    F(\vec q^{\,2}) \;=\; \int \dd^{3}r\;\rho(\vec r)\,
    \mathrm{e}^{\,\mathrm{i}\vec q\cdot\vec r/\hbar}.
    \label{eq:form-factor-def}
\end{equation}
A pointlike charge, $\rho(\vec r)=\delta^{3}(\vec r)$, gives a constant form factor and
recovers the Mott result at all $Q^{2}$; any spatial spread makes $F$ fall with
increasing $Q^{2}$, and the shape of that fall-off encodes the shape of the charge
distribution. The correspondence is qualitative but instructive: an exponential charge
distribution produces a dipole form factor (this is the case of the proton), a Gaussian
distribution produces a Gaussian form factor, and a sharp-edged homogeneous sphere
produces an oscillating form factor with diffraction minima. Reading the fall-off
backwards is, in principle, an inverse Fourier transform from the measured $F(Q^{2})$
to the charge density $\rho(\vec r)$ \cite[Ch.~1]{Amsler2018}. The caveat to this
intuitive reading---that the identification of a form factor with the Fourier transform
of a static density holds strictly only in the Breit frame, and that relativistic recoil
spoils it at large $Q^{2}$---is discussed by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ target such as the nucleon carries not one but two independent
form factors, because the virtual photon couples both to the charge and to the magnetic
moment. They are the \emph{Dirac form factor} $F_{1}(Q^{2})$, associated with the
convective (charge) current, and the \emph{Pauli form factor} $F_{2}(Q^{2})$,
associated with the spin-flip (anomalous-magnetic-moment) current. Their values at zero
momentum transfer are fixed by the static properties of the nucleon,
\begin{equation}
    F_{1}(0) = Q_{N}, \qquad F_{2}(0) = \kappa_{N},
    \label{eq:F1F2-norm}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{N}$ is the nucleon charge in units of $e$ (one for the proton, zero for the
neutron) and $\kappa_{N}$ is its anomalous magnetic moment. These normalizations are
the low-$Q^{2}$ anchors of the whole subject: any parametrization of the form factors
must reproduce Eq.~\eqref{eq:F1F2-norm} at the origin, and the \emph{slope} at the
origin measures the nucleon's size, as we shall see in
Sect.~\ref{subsec:proton-radius} \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The decomposition of the
nucleon matrix element of the electromagnetic current into the Dirac and Pauli terms,
and the normalizations \eqref{eq:F1F2-norm}, follow Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{The Rosenbluth separation and the Sachs form factors}
\label{subsec:rosenbluth}

To connect the two form factors to a measurable cross section one must combine the
Dirac and Pauli currents with the electron kinematics. The result, the
\emph{Rosenbluth formula}, expresses the elastic cross section as the Mott cross section
multiplied by a bracket that mixes the two form factors,
\begin{equation}
    \dv{\sigma}{\Omega}
    = \left(\dv{\sigma}{\Omega}\right)_{\!\mathrm{Mott}}
      \left[\frac{G_{E}^{2}(Q^{2}) + \tau\,G_{M}^{2}(Q^{2})}{1+\tau}
      + 2\tau\,G_{M}^{2}(Q^{2})\tan^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right],
    \qquad
    \tau = \frac{Q^{2}}{4M^{2}}.
    \label{eq:rosenbluth}
\end{equation}
It is written not in terms of $F_{1}$ and $F_{2}$ but in terms of the \emph{Sachs form
factors}, the linear combinations
\begin{align}
    G_{E}(Q^{2}) &= F_{1}(Q^{2}) - \tau\,F_{2}(Q^{2}), \label{eq:GE}\\
    G_{M}(Q^{2}) &= F_{1}(Q^{2}) + F_{2}(Q^{2}), \label{eq:GM}
\end{align}
which are chosen because the cross section \eqref{eq:rosenbluth} contains no
interference term between them: $G_{E}$ and $G_{M}$ appear only squared. In the
Breit frame $G_{E}$ and $G_{M}$ are the Fourier transforms of the charge and
magnetization distributions, respectively, which is why they, rather than $F_{1}$ and
$F_{2}$, are the natural objects to measure. Their normalizations follow from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:F1F2-norm},
\begin{equation}
    G_{E}(0) = Q_{N}, \qquad G_{M}(0) = \mu_{N},
    \label{eq:GEGM-norm}
\end{equation}
with $\mu_{N}$ the total magnetic moment of the nucleon in nuclear magnetons
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001} derive
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rosenbluth} and motivate the Sachs combinations
\eqref{eq:GE}--\eqref{eq:GM} by their Breit-frame interpretation as the charge and
magnetization distributions; the corresponding cross sections are collected in their
Appendix~B.

The structure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rosenbluth} suggests a clean experimental strategy,
the \emph{Rosenbluth separation}. At fixed $Q^{2}$ one divides the measured cross
section by the Mott cross section and plots the result against $\tan^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}$,
which requires taking data at several beam energies and scattering angles chosen to keep
$Q^{2}$ constant. The bracket in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rosenbluth} is then a straight line whose
slope is proportional to $G_{M}^{2}(Q^{2})$ and whose intercept fixes the combination
$[G_{E}^{2}+\tau G_{M}^{2}]/(1+\tau)$; the two together yield $G_{E}$ and $G_{M}$
separately at that $Q^{2}$. Repeating the exercise over a range of momentum transfers
maps out both form factors. The method has one intrinsic weakness that will matter
below: the electric term enters the intercept weighted by $1/(1+\tau)$ relative to the
magnetic term, so as $Q^{2}$ grows and $\tau$ becomes large the sensitivity to $G_{E}$
shrinks, and extracting it from the small residual $\tan^{2}\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ dependence
becomes increasingly difficult \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. This loss of sensitivity is
an intrinsic feature of the method rather than of any particular experiment, and it is
already noted as such by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Applied across a wide range of momentum transfers, the Rosenbluth method reveals a
remarkably simple regularity: all four nucleon form factors are described, to first
approximation, by a single universal \emph{dipole} shape,
\begin{equation}
    G_{D}(Q^{2}) = \left(1 + \frac{Q^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)^{\!-2},
    \qquad \Lambda^{2} = 0.71~\mathrm{GeV}^{2},
    \label{eq:dipole}
\end{equation}
in the sense that $G_{E}^{p}\approx G_{D}$ and $G_{M}^{p}/\mu_{p}\approx G_{D}$, and
similarly for the dominant neutron magnetic form factor. The dipole shape corresponds,
through Eq.~\eqref{eq:form-factor-def}, to an exponential charge distribution, and its
scale $\Lambda$ fixes the size of the nucleon. Figure~\ref{fig:dipole-sachs} shows the
proton electric and (normalized) magnetic form factors following this common curve.
The dipole is only an approximation---modern data show few-percent deviations that
carry real information---but it is an excellent organizing baseline and the reference
against which all departures are quoted \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The dipole
parametrization \eqref{eq:dipole}, its exponential-density interpretation and the
empirical scaling relations among the four nucleon form factors are discussed by Thomas
and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{Polarization transfer and the $G_E/G_M$ discrepancy}
\label{subsec:polarization-transfer}

The weakness of the Rosenbluth method at large $Q^{2}$---the shrinking weight of the
electric term---makes $G_{E}^{p}$ progressively harder to extract just where one most
wants to know it. When $\tau G_{M}^{2}\gg G_{E}^{2}$ the cross section is dominated by
the magnetic form factor and the electric contribution is a small correction on a large
background, so that percent-level systematic errors in the absolute cross section
translate into large errors in $G_{E}^{p}$. A different observable is needed, one in
which $G_{E}$ and $G_{M}$ enter on a more equal footing.

\emph{Polarization transfer} provides exactly this. One scatters a longitudinally
polarized electron off an unpolarized proton and measures the polarization of the
recoiling proton,
\begin{equation}
    \vec e + p \;\rightarrow\; e + \vec p,
\end{equation}
resolving it into a component $P_{t}$ transverse to and a component $P_{l}$ along the
proton momentum in the scattering plane. The ratio of the two recoil polarizations is,
remarkably, directly proportional to the ratio of the form factors,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_{E}}{G_{M}}
    = -\,\frac{P_{t}}{P_{l}}\,\frac{E+E'}{2M}\,\tan\tfrac{\theta}{2}.
    \label{eq:pol-transfer}
\end{equation}
Because $P_{t}$ arises from the electric--magnetic interference and $P_{l}$ from the
magnetic term, the ratio $G_{E}/G_{M}$ appears \emph{linearly} rather than through a
small squared difference, and it is measured as a ratio of two polarization components
in the same detector. A great many systematic uncertainties---beam intensity, target
density, detector acceptance---cancel in that ratio, so the method is exquisitely
sensitive to $G_{E}/G_{M}$ precisely in the region where the cross section is dominated
by $G_{M}$ and the Rosenbluth method fails. The technique requires a beam of known
helicity and a polarimeter for the recoil proton; a partial derivation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pol-transfer} from the hadronic tensor is given in Sect.~3.1 of
Perdrisat, Punjabi and Vanderhaeghen (Prog.\ Part.\ Nucl.\ Phys.\ 59 (2007) 694),
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}, and the polarization-transfer
formalism is also treated by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Measuring the recoil polarization is itself an experimental problem worth a sentence,
because it uses the same spin--orbit physics that pervaded the baryon-polarization
measurements of Chapter~\ref{sec:baryon-spectroscopy}. Proton polarimetry is based on
the spin--orbit interaction in a secondary scattering off an analyzer nucleus: the
$\vec L\cdot\vec S$ force produces a left--right asymmetry whose sign and magnitude
measure the transverse polarization component. The spin--orbit force is, however, blind
to the \emph{longitudinal} polarization, which must first be rotated into a transverse
direction; this is done by letting the proton precess in the magnetic field of the
spectrometer dipole before it reaches the polarimeter. The comparison of the front and
back tracking chambers on either side of the analyzer then yields both polarization
components \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

The physics payoff was a surprise. Rosenbluth separations had long suggested that the
proton electric and magnetic form factors keep the same shape, so that the ratio
$\mu_{p}\,G_{E}^{p}/G_{M}^{p}\approx 1$ is roughly independent of $Q^{2}$. The
polarization-transfer measurements at Jefferson Lab told a different story: the ratio
falls steadily and approximately linearly with increasing $Q^{2}$, dropping well below
unity by a few $\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$. The two methods, each internally consistent,
disagree---and the discrepancy grows with $Q^{2}$, exactly where the Rosenbluth
extraction of $G_{E}^{p}$ is least reliable. This is not a small effect at the edge of
the errors; it is a qualitative disagreement about whether the charge and magnetization
of the proton have the same spatial extent. The two leading explanations are that the
Rosenbluth cross sections need a radiative correction that had been underestimated, or
that the one-photon-exchange picture itself receives a non-negligible \emph{two-photon}
correction; the latter is the subject of the next subsection \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.
It should
be noted that the discrepancy itself emerged from the Jefferson Lab measurements of the
early 2000s and is therefore not yet reflected in Thomas and Weise
\cite{ThomasWeise2001}, which predates it.

\subsection{Two-photon exchange}
\label{subsec:two-photon}

The whole apparatus of form factors rests on the one-photon-exchange approximation,
and the $G_{E}/G_{M}$ discrepancy is the first place where that approximation is
seriously questioned. Exchanging a second photon between the electron and the proton
is suppressed by one power of $\alpha$ relative to the Born graph and had traditionally
been folded into the standard radiative corrections only in part. The two methods
respond to it differently: because the Rosenbluth intercept extracts a small electric
term from a large magnetic background, it is far more vulnerable to a small
angle-dependent two-photon contamination than the polarization-transfer ratio, which is
protected by the cancellation of systematics. A two-photon-exchange (TPE) contribution
of the expected size could therefore reconcile the two extractions
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The one-photon-exchange approximation that TPE corrects, and
the conditions under which it is expected to hold, are stated by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.1]{ThomasWeise2001}; the TPE programme itself post-dates that book.

The clean experimental signature of TPE is a comparison of electron and positron
scattering. Reversing the lepton charge flips the sign of the interference between the
one- and two-photon amplitudes, so the ratio
\begin{equation}
    R_{2\gamma}
    = \frac{\sigma_{e^{+}p}}{\sigma_{e^{-}p}}
    = \left(\frac{N_{\exp}(e^{+})}{N_{\exp}(e^{-})}\right)
      \Bigg/
      \left(\frac{N_{\mathrm{MC}}(e^{+})}{N_{\mathrm{MC}}(e^{-})}\right)
    \label{eq:r2gamma}
\end{equation}
deviates from unity by twice the TPE effect; the Monte-Carlo factors normalize away the
charge-independent radiative corrections. This is what the OLYMPUS experiment measured
at the DORIS storage ring at DESY, alternating $e^{-}$ and $e^{+}$ beams through the same
detector roughly once a day so that the two data sets were taken under nearly identical
conditions, which is essential because the effect sought is at the percent level.
The critical systematic is the relative luminosity of the two beams, and much of the
experimental effort went into measuring it \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

The outcome is that two-photon exchange is real but small, of the order of a percent
and rising toward backward angles (small $\varepsilon$, the virtual-photon longitudinal
polarization). Within the still-sizable uncertainties this is about the magnitude needed
to bring the Rosenbluth and polarization-transfer determinations of
$\mu_{p}\,G_{E}^{p}/G_{M}^{p}$ into agreement, so TPE is the currently favored resolution
of the discrepancy. It is not yet the last word: the measurements were confined to
$Q^{2}\lesssim 2.5~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$, whereas the discrepancy is largest at higher
$Q^{2}$, and closing that gap---perhaps with future measurements at Jefferson Lab---is
needed before the case can be considered settled \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

\subsection{The neutron form factor}
\label{subsec:neutron-ff}

Everything so far concerned the proton, but a complete picture of nucleon structure
requires the neutron, and the neutron poses its own difficulties. The first is that
there is no free neutron target: neutrons are unstable in isolation and must be studied
bound in a light nucleus, in practice the deuteron or helium-3, with the theoretical
complication of extracting the neutron response from the nuclear one. The second is that
the neutron is electrically neutral, so its electric form factor vanishes at the origin,
$G_{E}^{n}(0)=0$, and remains small at all momentum transfers; measuring a small quantity
that must be disentangled from nuclear effects is the central challenge of neutron
form-factor physics \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The extraction of the neutron form
factors from deuterium and $^{3}\mathrm{He}$ targets, and the nuclear corrections it
requires, are reviewed by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Despite its smallness, $G_{E}^{n}$ is not zero, and its non-vanishing is itself
structural information: it shows that the neutron has an internal charge separation even
though its net charge is zero. The measured $G_{E}^{n}$ rises to a small positive
maximum around $Q^{2}\sim 0.3$--$0.5~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$ and falls slowly thereafter. Read
through the Fourier correspondence of Eq.~\eqref{eq:form-factor-def}, this corresponds
to a spatial charge density that is positive in the interior and negative in the outer
region---consistent with the intuitive picture of the neutron's negatively charged
$d$-quark tail extending beyond a more compact positive core---so that the two
contributions cancel in the total charge but not in the spatial distribution. The
neutron \emph{magnetic} form factor $G_{M}^{n}$, by contrast, is large and follows the
same standard dipole shape as the proton form factors to good approximation, reflecting
the fact that the neutron's magnetic moment is comparable to the proton's
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The interpretation of a non-vanishing $G_{E}^{n}$ in terms
of an interior--exterior charge separation, and its natural explanation through the pion
cloud, are discussed by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.2, Ch.~7.4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

\subsection{The proton charge radius and the radius puzzle}
\label{subsec:proton-radius}

The most basic number one can extract from the elastic form factors is the size of the
proton, and it is the subject of one of the more instructive controversies of recent
subatomic physics. The charge radius is defined through the low-$Q^{2}$ slope of the
electric form factor. Expanding Eq.~\eqref{eq:form-factor-def} for small momentum
transfer gives
\begin{equation}
    G_{E}(Q^{2}) = 1 - \frac{r_{p}^{2}}{6}\,Q^{2} + \cdots,
    \label{eq:GE-expansion}
\end{equation}
so that the mean-square charge radius is minus six times the slope of $G_{E}$ at the
origin,
\begin{equation}
    r_{p}^{2} = -6\,\left.\dv{G_{E}}{Q^{2}}\right|_{Q^{2}=0}.
    \label{eq:rp-slope}
\end{equation}
The experimental task is therefore to measure the \emph{slope} of $G_{E}$ as $Q^{2}\to 0$,
which is intrinsically hard: one must map the form factor at the smallest accessible
momentum transfers, where it differs from unity by only a fraction of a percent, and
extrapolate the slope to the origin. The high-precision elastic-scattering measurement
at Mainz gave
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle r_{E}^{2}\right\rangle^{1/2}
    = 0.879(5)_{\mathrm{stat.}}(4)_{\mathrm{syst.}}(2)_{\mathrm{model}}(4)_{\mathrm{group}}
      ~\mathrm{fm},
    \label{eq:rp-mainz}
\end{equation}
where the last two uncertainties reflect the choice of fit function and of data
grouping---already a warning that the extrapolation carries model dependence
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The definition \eqref{eq:rp-slope} of the charge radius as
the slope of $G_{E}$ at the origin, and its relation to the low-$Q^{2}$ expansion of the
form factor, are given by Thomas and Weise \cite[Ch.~2.2]{ThomasWeise2001}.

A completely independent route to the same number uses atomic spectroscopy rather than
scattering. An electron in an $S$ state of hydrogen has a non-vanishing probability of
being found \emph{inside} the proton, and the finite proton size shifts the energy of
that state; an electron in a $P$ state has a node at the origin and is essentially
unaffected. Measuring $P\!-\!S$ transition frequencies to very high precision therefore
determines the proton size, once the pure QED contributions---the Bohr levels, the Dirac
fine structure, the Lamb shift and the hyperfine splitting---have been subtracted. Many
such transitions in ordinary hydrogen were measured before 2010, and their weighted
average agreed, within errors, with the electron-scattering value; the two independent
methods appeared to give a consistent proton radius of about $0.88~\mathrm{fm}$
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

The consensus broke in 2010. The muon is about two hundred times heavier than the
electron, so in \emph{muonic} hydrogen the muon orbits some two hundred times closer to
the proton and spends far more of its time inside it; the finite-size shift of the muonic
$S$ levels is enhanced by roughly the cube of the mass ratio, making muonic hydrogen an
exquisitely sensitive proton-size probe. The measurement is correspondingly harder---one
must produce a muon beam, stop it to form muonic hydrogen before the muon decays, and
perform laser spectroscopy on the resulting atom within microseconds---but the CREMA
collaboration succeeded, and their proton radius came out several standard deviations
\emph{smaller} than the electron-based value, near $0.84~\mathrm{fm}$. This
$\sim 4\%$ disagreement, far larger than the quoted uncertainties of either method, is
the \emph{proton radius puzzle}. Figure~\ref{fig:proton-radius-puzzle} compiles the
determinations that define it: the muonic-hydrogen values cluster tightly around the
small radius, while the older electron-scattering and hydrogen-spectroscopy values
scatter around the larger one \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

    \centering

             $r_{p}$ from electron--proton scattering ($ep$), ordinary hydrogen

             in the lecture and should be updated against the latest data.}
    \label{fig:proton-radius-puzzle}
\end{figure}

Since then the situation has moved, though not to everyone's satisfaction. Several newer
hydrogen-spectroscopy measurements and a dedicated low-$Q^{2}$ electron-scattering
experiment (PRad at Jefferson Lab) have come out closer to the smaller,
muonic-hydrogen value, and the 2018 CODATA adjustment shifted the recommended radius
downward accordingly, so that the discrepancy is now widely regarded as easing rather
than as a signal of new physics. But the individual determinations still scatter beyond
their quoted errors, and the underlying difficulty---that every scattering extraction
rests on an extrapolation of the slope \eqref{eq:rp-slope} to $Q^{2}=0$, with its
attendant model dependence---has not gone away \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.

The natural way to attack the extrapolation problem is to reduce the radiative
corrections that plague electron measurements, and here muons offer a second advantage
beyond their sensitivity: scattering measurements with muons need much smaller radiative
corrections than those with electrons, because the larger muon mass suppresses the
radiative tail. The drawback is that muon beams are unstable and hard to prepare, which
is why such a measurement was not attempted earlier. It is now the goal of the AMBER
experiment at CERN, which will use the versatile M2 beamline to deliver both positive and
negative muons onto an active hydrogen target and measure the forward-going scattered
muons in a spectrometer, the recoil proton being detected in a high-pressure hydrogen
time-projection chamber. Because the existing data sets agree near $Q^{2}=0$ but diverge
as $Q^{2}$ grows, and because the radius is the \emph{slope} at the origin, what is
needed is precisely a consistent map of $G_{E}$ across a wide range of small $Q^{2}$;
AMBER is designed to provide it \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The puzzle itself broke in
2010 and is therefore absent from Thomas and Weise \cite{ThomasWeise2001}, whose
treatment supplies the underlying form-factor formalism but not this controversy.

\subsection{Resonance electroproduction and transition form factors}
\label{subsec:transition-ff}

Elastic scattering probes the ground state of the nucleon, but the same virtual photon
can also \emph{excite} the nucleon, and stepping up in $W$ from the elastic point
$W=M$ into the resonance region turns electron scattering into a tool for the physics of
Chapter~\ref{sec:baryon-spectroscopy}. Increasing the energy transfer $\nu$ at fixed
$Q^{2}$ raises the invariant mass \eqref{eq:W2} of the hadronic system, and when $W$
matches the mass of a nucleon resonance the cross section shows a peak. The reaction
\begin{equation}
    ep \to e\Delta^{+} \to e\,p\pi^{0} \quad\text{or}\quad e\,n\pi^{+},
\end{equation}
for example, proceeds through the $\Delta(1232)$, and in the language of the exchanged
photon it is the excitation $\gamma^{*}p\to\Delta^{+}\to p\pi^{0}$ or $n\pi^{+}$. A
measurement of the inclusive electron spectrum $\mathrm{d}^{2}\sigma/\mathrm{d}\Omega\,
\mathrm{d}E'$ at fixed beam energy shows, as $E'$ decreases from the elastic peak, a
succession of broad bumps at the masses of the low-lying resonances---the
$\Delta(1232)$, then the second and third resonance regions---sitting on a smooth
background, before the spectrum turns into the featureless deep-inelastic continuum at
still lower $E'$ \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. Thomas and Weise devote a dedicated section
to the nucleon resonances seen in this way \cite[Ch.~2.3]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The strength of each resonance peak depends on $Q^{2}$, and this dependence is the new
structural information. As $Q^{2}$ increases the resonance bumps do not merely shrink
uniformly; their relative heights change, because each resonance is excited by the
photon with its own $Q^{2}$-dependent strength. Comparing the resonance region at
$Q^{2}=0$ (real-photon photoproduction), at a few tenths of a $\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$, and at
$Q^{2}\sim 1$--$2~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}$ shows the $\Delta(1232)$ dominating at low $Q^{2}$
and falling faster than the higher resonances as $Q^{2}$ grows. This $Q^{2}$ dependence
is quantified by \emph{transition form factors}, the analogues of the elastic form
factors for an excitation. Formally, elastic scattering measures the diagonal matrix
element of the electromagnetic current between ground states,
$\gamma^{*}N\to N\Rightarrow\ket{N|J^{\mu}|N}$, whereas resonance excitation measures the
\emph{off-diagonal} element between the ground state and an excited state,
$\gamma^{*}N\to N^{*}\Rightarrow\ket{N^{*}|J^{\mu}|N}$. The elastic form factors describe
the structure of the ground state; the transition form factors describe how the nucleon
is promoted into a specific higher state, and thereby test the wavefunction of that
excited state directly \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The matrix-element definition of the
transition form factors and their multipole decomposition follow Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.3]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The number of independent transition form factors is fixed by the spins involved. A
transition between two spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ states, $N\to N$, needs two form factors,
$G_{E}$ and $G_{M}$, as in the elastic case. A transition from a spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$
nucleon to the spin-$\tfrac{3}{2}$ $\Delta$, $N\to\Delta$, needs three: a magnetic
dipole $G_{M}^{*}$, an electric quadrupole $G_{E}^{*}$ and a Coulomb quadrupole
$G_{C}^{*}$, corresponding to the transition multipoles $M1$, $E2$ and $C2$
respectively. Experimentally one does not measure these form factors directly but
extracts, from the angular distributions and polarization observables of the decay
products, a set of \emph{helicity amplitudes}---$A_{1/2}$, $A_{3/2}$ and
$S_{1/2}$---which are then related to the form factors. This is the same helicity-amplitude
language introduced for photoproduction in Chapter~\ref{sec:baryon-spectroscopy}, now
extended to virtual photons and hence to non-zero $Q^{2}$ \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}.
The relation between the helicity amplitudes $A_{1/2}$, $A_{3/2}$, $S_{1/2}$ and the
multipoles $M1$, $E2$, $C2$ is set out by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.3]{ThomasWeise2001}.

The $\Delta(1232)$ illustrates what such measurements reveal. Using a polarized electron
beam and a polarized target, an experiment like CLAS at Jefferson Lab measures a set of
beam and target asymmetries, fits them to extract the helicity amplitudes, and from
these computes the three transition form factors as functions of $Q^{2}$. Two results
stand out. First, the dominant magnetic dipole form factor $G_{M}^{*}$ falls off
noticeably faster with $Q^{2}$ than the elastic proton magnetic form factor, showing
that the spatial structure of the $N\to\Delta$ transition differs from that of the
ground state. Second, it is common to quote the small quadrupole amplitudes as ratios to
the dominant dipole,
\begin{equation}
    R_{EM} = \frac{E_{1+}}{M_{1+}}, \qquad R_{SM} = \frac{S_{1+}}{M_{1+}},
    \label{eq:multipole-ratios}
\end{equation}
because these ratios test the shape of the transition directly. In the simplest quark
model the $N\to\Delta$ transition is a pure spin flip of a single quark, a pure $M1$
transition, which predicts
\begin{equation}
    R_{EM} = R_{SM} = 0.
    \label{eq:naive-quark-prediction}
\end{equation}
The data disagree: both ratios are small but non-zero, with $R_{EM}$ at the level of a
few percent and $R_{SM}$ growing in magnitude with $Q^{2}$. A non-vanishing quadrupole
means that the $\Delta$, or the transition to it, is not spherically symmetric---a
deformation that the naive single-quark picture cannot produce and that points to
$D$-state admixtures or pion-cloud effects in the nucleon wavefunction. This connects
directly to the residual-interaction discussion of
Sect.~\ref{subsec:qm-predictions}: the transition form factors are a quantitative test
of the same quark-model dynamics that the mass spectrum probes only indirectly
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The deformation implied by a non-vanishing $R_{EM}$, and in
particular the role of the pion cloud in generating it, connect directly to the chiral
treatment of the nucleon given by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.3, Ch.~7.4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Beyond the $\Delta$, transition form factors have become a systematic tool for
disentangling the nature of the nucleon resonances. The Roper resonance
$N(1440)\tfrac{1}{2}^{+}$ is the classic problem case: its position below its
negative-parity partners inverts the ordering expected from the quark model, and it has
long been debated whether it is an ordinary radial excitation, a hybrid with excited
glue, or a dynamically generated meson--baryon state. The $Q^{2}$ dependence of its
helicity amplitudes $A_{1/2}$ and $S_{1/2}$---in particular the sign change of $A_{1/2}$
at low $Q^{2}$ and its approach to the quark-model curve at high $Q^{2}$---has been
decisive evidence that the Roper is, after all, dominated by a three-quark core dressed
by a substantial meson cloud, rather than a pure hybrid. More generally, comparing the
transition form factors of resonances of different orbital, spin-flavor and radial
structure lets one read off which degrees of freedom are excited, how large the
meson-cloud contributions are, and how the description changes from the low-$Q^{2}$
hadronic regime to the high-$Q^{2}$
quark regime \cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The meson-cloud contributions that dominate the
low-$Q^{2}$ behaviour of these amplitudes are treated in the chiral framework of Thomas
and Weise \cite[Ch.~7.4]{ThomasWeise2001}.

Transition form factors are also accessible in the \emph{timelike} region, $q^{2}>0$,
through the radiative decays of hyperons and mesons into a lepton pair, which probe the
same matrix elements at $Q^{2}<0$ and provide a complementary handle on the electromagnetic
structure of excited states; the timelike region, its analytic connection to the spacelike
form factors and the role of the vector mesons there are treated by Thomas and Weise
\cite[Ch.~2.2.7]{ThomasWeise2001}. Taken together, the elastic and transition form
factors of
this chapter exhaust what can be learned while the nucleon or its excitation remains a
coherent object described by a small number of form factors. Pushing to still larger $W$
and $Q^{2}$ breaks the nucleon into its constituents and replaces form factors by parton
distributions: the transition from the hadronic to the quark-level description, sketched
qualitatively at the end of this chapter through the elastic $\to$ inelastic $\to$ deep
inelastic progression, is where Chapter~\ref{sec:dis-nucleon-structure} begins
\cite[Ch.~14]{Amsler2018}. The same progression organizes Thomas and Weise, whose
Chapter~2 on the electromagnetic structure of the nucleon gives way to the deep
inelastic scattering of their Chapter~4 \cite[Ch.~2, Ch.~4]{ThomasWeise2001}.
